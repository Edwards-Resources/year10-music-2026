Lesson 20: Music of Australia and First Nations music

Lesson 20 of 28, in Week 8 of Sound Detectives.

Learning intention

I can describe Australian music and its context respectfully.

Success criteria

tick them off as you go, they save on your device

Read this first

There is no single Aboriginal sound. This music belongs to particular people, in a particular place.

Before you start: this page carries the names, voices and images of Aboriginal people who have died. Under Yolŋu protocol the name of someone who has died is usually not used at all. Gurrumul's family gave permission for his name and image to be used publicly again after his final funeral ceremony, so that his music could keep reaching people. That decision was theirs to make and nobody else's, and it is the clearest example you will get of what a protocol actually is: a rule that belongs to a Community, about their own Cultural material.

Music of Australia is a category, not a style

Music of Australia is one of the required areas of study in this course, and it covers everything made here: First Nations music, and the music of everyone who came afterwards. There are hundreds of First Nations across this continent, each with their own Country, Languages and Cultural practices, so there is no one “Aboriginal sound” any more than there is one “European sound”. Every artist on this page is Yolŋu, from north-east Arnhem Land. That makes them a good set to compare with each other, and a bad set to generalise from.

The yidaki is not a national instrument

The instrument usually called a didgeridoo has its own name, the yidaki, in the Yolŋu languages of north-east Arnhem Land. “Didgeridoo” is an introduced English word, not the instrument's own. It comes from the north of the continent rather than all of it, so it does not belong in a description of music from everywhere in Australia, and you will not hear it in every work on this page. Who may play it is decided by the Traditional Owners of the place it comes from, and those protocols differ between Communities. There is no single national rule, and the answer for any particular Community belongs to that Community.

What is yours to say, and what is not

  • Describe the music. Language, instruments, texture, rhythm, structure, the way a traditional element sits inside a contemporary one. This is what you are assessed on and it is yours to describe, exactly as in any other work.
  • Use context the artists have made public themselves. Baker Boy raps in Yolŋu Matha and in English, and “Marryuna” means dance. King Stingray call their own sound Yolŋu surf rock. That is context straight from the artist, and it is safe ground.
  • Do not invent the meaning. If nobody has said what a song is about, writing that is a better answer than guessing at it. “The artists have not made that public” is a real sentence and a respectable one.

Working rule: describe what you hear, and attribute what you know. The question is still how the elements of music are used. Context earns marks when it is accurate and sourced. Decoration earns nothing, and guessing at Cultural meaning is worse than nothing.

What we are doing

  1. Discuss what we mean by music of Australia, including First Nations music.
  2. Listen with respect and attention to context (cultural, historical, social).
  3. Note the instruments, language and stories where shared by the artists.
  4. Connect features of the music to its context.

Your task

For one work, describe two musical features and explain how they connect to the music’s context.

Before you leave

One thing you learned about the context of an Australian work.
