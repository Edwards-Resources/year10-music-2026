Lesson 14: Jazz listening

Lesson 14 of 28, in Week 6 of Sound Detectives.

Read this

Nobody wrote the solo down. That is the whole point, and you can hear it once you know what to listen for.

Jazz grew out of African American communities in the southern United States around the turn of the twentieth century, with New Orleans as its best-known starting point and the blues and ragtime as its roots. From there it spread through the dance bands of the 1930s and 40s and then into the small, fast, virtuosic groups of bebop. What holds all of that together is one habit: part of the music is invented while it is being played.

Four things that make it sound like jazz

  • Swing. The beat is divided unevenly, long then short, instead of into equal halves. It is the difference between music you march to and music you nod to, and it is felt rather than notated.
  • Improvisation. Parts are made up in performance over an agreed chord pattern. Not random: the form and the chords are fixed and everyone is counting. Only the notes on top are free.
  • Blue notes. Certain notes are flattened or bent slightly below where a major scale would put them. That deliberate sourness against the chord underneath is where a lot of the ache in the sound comes from.
  • The instrumentation. A rhythm section (piano or guitar, bass, drums) holds the form steady, while a front line (trumpet, saxophone, trombone, or a voice) takes turns on top. Listen for that split and the rest gets easier to follow.

How to tell an improvised section from a written one, which is your exit question: written ensemble passages have everyone locked together, arriving and stopping at the same instant, as in the Glenn Miller. When a solo starts, the texture thins to one voice on top while the rhythm section keeps doing exactly what it was already doing. Solo phrases start and end in awkward places, breathe unevenly, and would come out different if the band played the tune again tomorrow.

What we are doing

  1. Learn the context: where and when jazz developed.
  2. Meet the key features: swing, improvisation, blue notes, instrumentation.
  3. Listen to the three excerpts and spot these features.
  4. Identify the instruments and where the improvised solos are.
