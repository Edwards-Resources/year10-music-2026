Lesson 16: Analysing a jazz recording

Lesson 16 of 28, in Week 6 of Sound Detectives.

Learning intention

I can analyse how a jazz recording's register and dynamics change across a listening, using measured evidence.

Success criteria

tick them off as you go, they save on your device

Key word

Register

Which part of the pitch range - low, middle or high - carries most of the weight of the sound at a given moment. A recording's register can shift across a piece even when nothing else about it changes.

Read this

This recording barely changes from one second to the next - so stop listening for a sudden moment, and start listening for a slow drift.

Every element you have studied so far mostly announces itself moment to moment: a chord changes, a section drops out, a beat lands. This recording still has moments like that, but its biggest change is not one of them. It is a slow drift in register - which part of the pitch range carries the weight of the sound - that takes minutes to complete rather than seconds. The test below is how you catch a drift like that.

The register test

Two moves, and both work on any recording, not just this one. First, try to sing or hum along and notice where that takes your voice: are you straining upward, or growling down low? That tells you roughly where the tune itself sits. Second, and this is the more useful test for a recording like this one: imagine cutting everything below your own lowest comfortable note. If that loses nearly the whole sound, the recording is bottom-heavy. If you would barely notice, the weight has moved up and out of the bottom.

Working rule: do not wait for a loud or sudden moment. Run the cut-the-bottom test every minute or so, and compare the answer now with the answer a minute or two ago, not a second ago.

How this page measures it

Every proportion in this guide comes from the same starting point: split the recording, instant by instant, into bands from the very bottom of the pitch range to the very top, and ask what share of the whole - out of 1 - each band is carrying. A recording that is 'four fifths bottom-heavy' at a given second is one where four out of every five parts of that whole sit in the bottom two bands. That is the only measurement behind this page, and every figure below is translated into a proportion you can picture rather than left as a raw number.

There is a second measurement behind this page, and it is a different one: how much the recording's texture changes from one instant to the next, on a scale where a track that changes a great deal scores high and one that barely changes scores low. Measured this way, this recording is far calmer than the pop tracks already studied in this course: even at its most turbulent moment, its frame-to-frame change stays under half of the calmest moment either “Valerie” (Lesson 6) or “Watermelon Sugar” (Lesson 1) ever reaches. That does not mean nothing happens here. It means the changes are not on the surface: they happen underneath, over minutes, in which part of the range is doing the work.

The pulse, briefly

There is a pulse under all of this, at about 136 beats a minute, but it is a soft one: a measurement that finds a pop track's beat easily only just clears its usability threshold here. If you try to count along and lose the thread, that is not a failure of your ear. This recording's pulse is felt rather than stamped out.

Excerpt

Miles Davis, “So What”1959 · 9:24 · Miles Davis

The full recording runs 9:24, longer than any excerpt in the listening examination and longer than a class will sit through end to end. This guide works through the opening two minutes ten seconds, where the register moves from the bottom of the range to the middle, then picks the recording back up for its last ninety seconds, where that move runs in reverse. The five and a half minutes in between, where most of the recording's improvising happens, is skipped: nothing measured here pins down what happens through it, so nothing is written about it.

  1. 0:00The recording opens almost entirely in the bottom of the range: quiet, and heavy, with next to nothing above it. Run the cut-the-bottom test here and you would lose nearly the whole sound.
  2. 0:02The level does not sit still through this opening: it lurches up and down in sudden steps, while the register stays anchored down in the bottom of the range.
    1. 0:02First lurch, doubling the level in an instant.
    2. 0:14The biggest lurch in the whole opening: nearly four times as loud as a moment before.
    3. 0:19The sharpest change in harmony on the entire recording happens right here, while the sound is still quiet and in the bottom of the range. A big change is not always a loud one.
    4. 0:30This is the most bottom-heavy the recording ever gets: about four out of every five parts of what's sounding sit down in the bottom of the range. Run the cut-the-bottom test and you would lose nearly everything.
    5. 0:34The level drops back to a little over half what it just was, and the opening keeps living in the bottom of the range.
  3. 0:50Something starts to enter higher up. The share of the sound sitting above the middle of the range has been climbing steadily since 0:20, and from here it keeps climbing.
    1. 0:50More than five times what it was thirty seconds ago.
    2. 0:55Barely moves - it is holding the ground it just gained.
    3. 1:00Keeps climbing, and from here it stays higher than anywhere earlier in the recording. Meanwhile the bottom eases off: the share sitting in the bass and sub-bass falls to around half, or a little under, of what it read at its most bottom-heavy, thirty seconds into the track.
  4. 1:31The single biggest jump in level in the whole first four minutes: the loudness very nearly triples in about five seconds, and the middle of the range climbs by more than half in the same moment, so this arrival is felt in the body of the sound, not just on the level meter.
  5. 2:10By now the balance has flipped completely: the middle of the range carries most of the weight, and the bottom, which held about four fifths of everything a hundred seconds earlier, has emptied out to almost nothing. Run the cut-the-bottom test now and you would barely notice; a hundred seconds earlier you would have lost nearly the whole sound. That is the biggest change in this recording that can actually be measured, and it takes two minutes to happen, not one moment.
  6. 7:40Picking the recording back up for its last ninety seconds: from here it winds down rather than stopping. The level here is already down to a bit over half of what it read at 2:10, the last point earlier in this guide.
    1. 8:00Down again, to about three quarters of what it was twenty seconds before.
    2. 8:20The sound has dropped to under half what it was at 7:40, and what is left is increasingly bottom-heavy - about a quarter of it now sits in the bass.
    3. 8:50The level steadies rather than falling further, but the bottom keeps its grip: getting on for a quarter of the sound is still sitting in the bass.
    4. 9:10Down again, to about two thirds of what it just was, and the bass share keeps climbing - now close to double what it was at the start of this run of cues. As everything else drops away, the bottom of the range is what's left standing, which is also where the whole recording began.

Notice what these cues never do: not one of them names an instrument. A measurement can say how loud a moment is and which part of the range is sounding, but it cannot say what is making that sound, so this page leaves that job to your ears. It never says who is soloing either, for the same reason. This guide stops at 9:10; past that point the recording keeps thinning, and by the very end there is next to nothing left.

What we are doing

  1. Listen to the opening two minutes ten seconds of “So What”, and run the cut-the-bottom test as you follow the guide.
  2. Read how the recording's register drifts across that span, and why the moment-to-moment surface stays so calm.
  3. Pick the recording back up for its last ninety seconds and run the same test in reverse.
  4. Work through the model answer, then write your own register-shift claim with evidence.
  5. Check your answers against the self-check.

Your task

In two or three sentences, describe how the register of “So What” changes across the span you listened to today. Use at least one timestamp and one proportion from the guide as evidence (for example, 'about half', 'nearly all', 'a quarter').

Worked answer

Describe how the register of this recording changes between the opening and 2:10. (1 mark)

This is the kind of claim a listening question rewards: a change, a location, and what it does. Here is one full mark, using the register test on the guide above.

At 0:30 the recording is at its most bottom-heavy: about four out of every five parts of what's sounding sit down in the bottom of the range, so cutting the low end here would lose almost the whole sound. By 2:10 that has reversed: the middle of the range now carries most of the weight and the bottom has emptied out to almost nothing, so the same recording that opened dark and heavy now sits open and central.

  • IdentifyName the change and say where it happens. The balance of the register flips from the bottom to the middle between 0:30 and 2:10. “The sound changes around the middle” names nothing and earns nothing.
  • DescribeAdd what the cut-the-bottom test would tell you at each point. At 0:30, cutting the low end loses nearly the whole sound. By 2:10, cutting the low end would barely be noticed, because the middle now carries the weight.
  • ExplainSay what it does to the recording. The track opens dark and bottom-heavy and gradually opens out as the middle takes over, which is the biggest single change in how the recording sits across its length.

What scores nothing

“The sound changes a bit around the middle.”

True and worth nothing: it does not say what changed, where it happens, or what effect it has. No timestamp, no direction, no proportion - there is nothing here to mark.

Before you leave

Describe one way the register of this recording changes between its opening and its ending.

Check yourself: four questions on today
  1. How does the register of the recording change between 0:30 and 2:10? It flips: the sound goes from almost entirely in the bottom of the range to having the middle carry most of the weight, while the bottom empties out to almost nothing.
  2. What happens to the register over the recording's last ninety seconds? It moves back towards the bottom: as the overall level falls, the proportion of sound sitting in the bass keeps growing, so the low end is the last thing still sounding, the same part of the range the recording opened in.
  3. About how fast is the pulse in this recording, and is it easy to lock onto? About 136 beats a minute, and no, it is a soft pulse that takes real effort to count, unlike a pop track's pulse.
  4. Frame to frame, does this recording change more or less than the pop tracks studied earlier in the course? Far less: even at its most turbulent moment, its frame-to-frame change stays under half of the calmest moment either pop track reaches.
