Lesson 26: Synthesis presentation

Lesson 26 of 28, in Week 10 of Sound Detectives.

Learning intention

I can present a clear analysis of a work.

Success criteria

tick them off as you go, they save on your device

Read this

A spoken analysis is not a written one read aloud: the excerpt carries the evidence, so your voice only has to carry the effect.

You have about two or three minutes as a group, and a work has six elements and hundreds of moments where any one of them could be doing something interesting. You cannot say everything, so the actual skill in this lesson is choosing what to leave out, not finding something to say.

Choosing what to say

  • Listen through the whole work once as a group and log every moment where an element is clearly doing something, the way you have in every listening lesson this term.
  • From that list, keep the two or three moments where the element is most audible and most decisive for the piece, not the two or three you personally find easiest to talk about. A subtle change you can defend beats an obvious one you can only describe.
  • For each moment you keep, decide the shortest excerpt that lets the class hear the change for themselves: a few seconds either side of the boundary, not the whole section leading up to it.

Working rule: if you cannot say in one sentence why a moment made your shortlist, it should not be on it. A moment earns its place because it does something decisive to the piece, not because it is easy to point at.

What changes when you speak it instead of writing it

Lesson 3 and Lesson 9 built the four-part response sentence: name the element, say how it is used, say what effect that has, then give your evidence. All four parts still apply here, but the last one changes shape. In writing, your evidence is a timestamp someone else has to go and check. Live, you do not cite the moment, you play it: the excerpt is the evidence, sitting right there in front of the class, which means every word you say afterwards can go straight to the effect instead of describing what people just heard for themselves.

That is also why a viva voce is harder than it sounds. Nobody hands you a script to read from, and the questions that follow a presentation are usually about the moment you chose, not the ones you left out. You need to know why you kept it well enough to defend that choice on the spot, not because you memorised an answer, but because you actually ran the shortlisting steps above.

What we are doing

  1. In your group, choose one work you can find a clean audio source for and that has clear, audible moments in at least two elements.
  2. Listen through once as a group and shortlist the two or three most decisive moments, using the procedure above.
  3. For each moment, cut the shortest excerpt that proves it and write a four-part response sentence (name, how used, effect, evidence).
  4. Plan who cues the audio and who speaks, and rehearse the handover so there is no dead air between the excerpt and the spoken effect.
  5. Present to the class.
  6. After each other group presents, give them one specific strength and one question, naming a moment rather than a general impression.

Your task

Present your group's analysis of how two or three elements create interest in your chosen work, using a short audio excerpt as evidence for each one and speaking the effect rather than reading it. If you were away, run the shortlisting procedure alone on a work of your own choice: cut two or three excerpts, then record yourself playing each one and speaking its effect straight after it.

Check yourself: presenting live
  1. Which one of the four Listening and Investigation criteria is this lesson mainly rehearsing, and how many of the 20 marks is it worth? Presentation and communication (multimodal or viva voce), worth 5 marks. The other three criteria are rehearsed elsewhere in this course, not by a live audience today.
  2. What is the difference between playing an excerpt as evidence and playing it as decoration? Evidence is cued to the exact moment the effect happens and is followed by the spoken effect; decoration is played for atmosphere with nothing specific said about it afterwards.
  3. What does turning a written response sentence into something you say live actually look like? Written: 'the texture thins to solo voice at the bridge, which pulls focus onto the words, evidenced by the drop from full band to one voice.' Spoken: play four or five seconds around that drop, then say only the effect out loud, because the audio has already given your evidence. The four-part shape does not change. Which part carries the evidence does.
  4. Can this page tell you which moments your group should present, or what you should say about them? No - that depends entirely on the work your group chose, which nobody can know in advance. This page can hand back the fixed facts: the rubric, the procedure, and the mark allocation. The moments themselves, and what you say about them, are your group's own.
  5. If you were not in the room to hear another group present, how do you run today's real test on yourself? Play back your own recording, choose one excerpt, and check whether it was cued to the exact moment the effect happens and whether you spoke the effect straight after it or left dead air first. The test is the same one the lesson teaches; you are just running it on your own work instead of someone else's.

Before you leave

Name one specific moment another group used well, and say why it worked as evidence rather than decoration. If you were not there to hear another group, run the same test on your own recording from today's activity instead.
