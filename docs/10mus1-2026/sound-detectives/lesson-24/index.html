Lesson 24

Lesson 24: Critical listening: evaluating choices

Lesson 24 of 28, in Week 9 of Sound Detectives.

Learning intention

I can evaluate interpretive choices in performance.

Success criteria

tick them off as you go, they save on your device

Read this

Evaluate means make a judgement and defend it. Liking one better is not a judgement.

Every listening task so far has asked you to analyse: how is this music put together, and what does that do. This one asks you to evaluate, which is a step up. You have to commit to a verdict about which interpretation works better, and then make someone else agree with you using nothing but the music.

What an evaluation has that an analysis does not

  • A judgement you commit to. Not “both are good in their own way”. Pick one. Sitting on the fence reads as not having heard a difference.
  • A stated criterion. More effective at what? Building tension, carrying the words, suiting the venue, serving the composer's marking. You have to say, because that is the standard you are judging against.
  • Evidence, same as always. The tempo, dynamics, expression or timbre choice that actually made the difference.

Working rule: name your criterion or the judgement is just taste. Two performances of the same work are not better or worse in the abstract, and a marker cannot give credit for a preference. One is more effective at something, and the moment you name that something, the evidence you need becomes obvious.

Listen

Vivaldi, 'Spring' (traditional recording, already analysed in Lesson 10).

Listen

'Recomposed by Max Richter: Vivaldi, The Four Seasons: Spring 1'. A radically different interpretive take on the same material.

What we are doing

  1. Listen to both versions of the work.
  2. Note the differences in tempo, dynamics, expression and timbre.
  3. Decide which you find more effective and why.
  4. Justify your judgement with musical evidence.

Your task

Write a short evaluation: which version is more effective, and what musical evidence supports your view?

Before you leave

Name one interpretive choice that changed the feel of the work.
