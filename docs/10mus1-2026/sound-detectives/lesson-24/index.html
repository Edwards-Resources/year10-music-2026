Lesson 24: Critical listening: evaluating choices

Lesson 24 of 28, in Week 9 of Sound Detectives.

Learning intention

I can evaluate interpretive choices in performance.

Success criteria

tick them off as you go, they save on your device

Read this

Liking a version is not evaluating it - naming the change, and judging it against a stated standard, is.

Every listening task so far has asked you to analyse: how is this music put together, and what does that do. This one asks you to evaluate, which is harder. You have to commit to a judgement about which of two performances works better, and defend it with nothing but the music.

The move, and it is only three steps

  • Name what changed. Not “they sound different” - say which measurable thing moved: how often the level steps, how low it sits, how thick the bottom of the sound is, how fast the surface changes.
  • Say what that change does. Not the number again in different words - the effect it has on a listener: it holds tension longer, it sits you somewhere lower, it never lets the level settle.
  • Only then, judge it against a stated standard. More effective at what? Building tension, holding contrast, keeping a listener inside one steady mood. Name the standard, then say which recording meets it better, and why.

Working rule: name your criterion before you name your verdict, or the verdict is only a preference wearing an argument's clothes. “I like the Richter better” names nothing that changed, does nothing with it, and answers a question nobody asked. Run all three moves and a marker who disagrees with your verdict still has to give you credit, because the moves are what earn it, not the conclusion.

The evidence

Four measured differences between these two recordings, one measured sameness, and not one of them says which is better.

Two recordings of the same material, both measured the same way. Four differences came back, plus one surprising sameness, and each one is something you can point at and time, which is what a judgement built on evidence actually needs.

  • How often the level moves. The Vivaldi performance changes its sustained level about eleven times a minute; the Richter, about five and a half. That holds whether you measure the whole Vivaldi recording or only the first three and a half minutes Richter recomposes, so it is a feature of the performance, not of the length.
  • How low it sits while it moves. Across that same span, the Vivaldi's level sits below a fixed quiet threshold for close to half the time measured; the Richter's sits there for under a third. The older performance is quieter for longer and reaches lower; the newer one sits higher and louder throughout.
  • Where the sound sits in the range. Once you leave out the quiet spots, about forty per cent more of the Richter's remaining sound sits below 250 Hz than the Vivaldi's does. More of the Richter's energy is low; more of the Vivaldi's is spread across the middle and top.
  • How fast the surface changes, instant to instant. The Richter's sound changes more between one analysis frame and the next than the Vivaldi's does, on average. More is happening moment to moment in the Richter, even though its level itself moves less often.
  • The sameness: tempo. Vivaldi: about 112 beats a minute. Richter: about 108. Within five beats a minute of each other. Whatever makes these two sound different, it is not speed - so tempo is not a difference to reach for.

Working rule: pick one of the four differences above as your evidence, not all four - and pick one the cues below give you a time for. The first two are cued on both recordings, so they are the ones you can point at. Where the sound sits in the range, and how fast its surface changes, are true of the whole recording rather than of any one second, so use them as context around your evidence rather than as the evidence itself. The fifth item, tempo, is not a difference at all - it is a measured sameness, and knowing that rules out one option rather than handing you one to use. A strong evaluation runs the three-move procedure once, on one measured difference, all the way to a verdict. A weak one lists every number on this page and judges none of them.

Excerpt 1: Vivaldi's Spring, the first movement

Antonio Vivaldi, The Four Seasons: “Spring” (La Primavera), RV 269, movement 111:09 · Voices of Music

This video is the whole three-movement concerto, but Richter's recomposition only rebuilds the first movement, so that is the only span worth comparing. Listen to the first three and a half minutes and stop there; the two movements that follow are not part of this comparison.

  1. 0:00Tap the beat as it starts. Counted properly, this recording sits at about 112 beats a minute, and when you get to the Richter you will find it within about five beats of that. Hold that number: whatever makes these two performances sound different, it is not going to be speed.
  2. 0:39The first big swing. In under a second the level drops to under half of what it just was - a listener who has only just settled into the sound is pulled out from under it without warning.
  3. 0:48And climbs back to nearly double, in well under ten seconds. That is the shape of this whole opening: not a fade, a series of steps.
  4. 1:03Down again, and further this time: to under a third of what it had been a moment before - the second time in under a minute the floor has dropped out from under a listener with no warning at all.
  5. 1:07And straight back up past double, only four seconds later. That is the fastest reversal in this span, and it is the single moment to point at if you want to argue from one event rather than from the rate: a listener who has just been dropped is hauled back up before they have settled into either.
  6. 1:26One more step up, one and a half times over - the fifth swing in under fifty seconds, and it is that running unpredictability, not any single swing, that you would argue from.
  7. 2:03The last, and largest, of the swings picked out for this span: more than double in one step. These six are only the largest named ones, over two minutes; counted properly across the whole first three and a half minutes, this recording steps its level up or down about eleven times a minute, more than twice as often as the Richter does across its own opening. What you have just heard is the shape behind that number, not the whole of it.

Stop the comparison at 3:30. Everything after this point in the video is the concerto's second and third movements, which Richter's recomposition never touches.

Excerpt 2: Richter's ‘Spring 1’

Max Richter, “Spring 1”, from Recomposed: Vivaldi's Four Seasons (2012)2012 · 2:32 · Max Richter

This recomposes the first movement you just listened to. It runs two and a half minutes against the excerpt's three and a half, so do not listen for the two to line up bar for bar. Listen for how differently each one moves through its own opening.

  1. 0:00The opening, and this is your zero. Quiet, and it barely moves for the first eight seconds: down slightly, if anything, not up. Hold this level in your ear, because every reading that follows is measured against it. And notice what has already happened by refusing to move: a recording that sits still for eight seconds is declining, from the first bar, to do the thing the Vivaldi does five times in fifty.
  2. 0:12By here the level has already stepped up by two-thirds in one move, and by a separate measure - how sharply the sound's colour changes from one instant to the next - this exact moment is the sharpest turn anywhere in the whole track.
  3. 0:20Climbing on: roughly twice the level it opened at, and the climb is not done yet, so a listener still gets no moment that feels like arrival.
  4. 0:28Higher again: more than three times the opening level now, and still no arrival - that only comes at the plateau eight seconds later.
  5. 0:36And here the climb stops. The level has barely moved in the last eight seconds: this recording reaches its level in one steady climb over about half a minute, then holds it, rather than stepping up and down the way the Vivaldi does.

Across its whole length this recording changes its sustained level about five and a half times a minute, against the Vivaldi's eleven. That difference is exactly what a steady climb like this one sounds like next to a run of sudden steps like the excerpt above.

What we are doing

  1. Read the three-move procedure for evaluating without just preferring.
  2. Listen to Vivaldi's Spring, first movement, using the cues, and stop at 3:30.
  3. Listen to Richter's ‘Spring 1’ using its cues.
  4. Pick one measured difference from The evidence to use as your own evidence.
  5. Write your evaluation using the three moves, then check it against the worked example.

Your task

Evaluate which recording is more effective, using the three-move procedure: name the measured difference you are using as evidence, with a time; say what it does to a listener; then judge it against a criterion you state yourself. Do not write “I like it better” anywhere in your answer.

Worked answer

Evaluate which recording is more effective at creating dramatic contrast. (4 marks)

This is what an evaluate question is asking for, and this lesson's activity asks the same thing in your own words. Here is one full-mark answer, built from the evidence above and one stated criterion. A different criterion, argued with the same three moves, would earn the same marks.

The Vivaldi recording is more effective at creating dramatic contrast across its opening two minutes. At 1:03 the level drops to under a third of what it had been a moment before, then climbs back past double it four seconds later at 1:07, a swing the Richter never attempts: its own opening does the opposite, climbing in one steady rise over about half a minute and then holding there. Measured across the whole of each recording, the Vivaldi changes its sustained level about eleven times a minute against the Richter's five and a half, so what happens at 1:03 to 1:07 is not an isolated moment but a running feature of the performance. That running unpredictability is what makes it more effective at holding a listener through sudden shifts in level, which is the standard this answer is judging against - a different standard, such as which recording sustains one steady mood for longer, would favour the Richter instead.

  • IdentifyName the recording and the criterion you are judging it against. The Vivaldi performance, for dramatic contrast. A verdict with no stated criterion is not yet a judgement, whichever way it goes.
  • DescribeGive the evidence, with a time. At 1:03 the level falls to under a third, then climbs past double it by 1:07; the Richter's opening manages one gradual climb over about half a minute instead. A marker should be able to hear it from your words without playing either track.
  • ExplainSay what the evidence does across the whole recording, not just the one moment. The Vivaldi changes its level about eleven times a minute against the Richter's five and a half, so this swing is a running feature, not a single lucky moment.
  • EvaluateState the verdict against the named criterion, and be honest that a different criterion could flip it. That makes the Vivaldi more effective at holding a listener through sudden shifts in level, though not necessarily at anything else.

What scores nothing

“I think the Richter version is better because it feels more emotional and modern.”

A preference, not a judgement. It names no measured moment on either recording, states no criterion beyond a feeling, and gives a marker nothing to check against the music. An evaluate question pays for the defence, not for which way the verdict falls, so this earns nothing at all.

Before you leave

What criterion did you judge by, and what timed evidence did you use to defend it?

Check yourself: four questions on today
  1. What are the three moves for evaluating a performance, in order? Name what changed, then say what that change does, then judge it against a stated standard. A verdict with no stated standard is a preference, not an evaluation.
  2. Vivaldi's Spring and Richter's ‘Spring 1’ sit within about five beats a minute of each other. So if the two sound different, what can you rule out as the cause? The tempo. Whatever the difference is, it is not speed.
  3. How often does each recording change its sustained level? About eleven times a minute for the Vivaldi against about five and a half for the Richter, so the Vivaldi moves roughly twice as often.
  4. Why does “I like the Richter better” earn no marks on an evaluate question? Because it names no measured evidence and no criterion: it states a preference, not a judgement.
