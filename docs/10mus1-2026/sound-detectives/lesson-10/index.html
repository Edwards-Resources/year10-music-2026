LearnExam strategy

You hear every excerpt three times. Each playing has one job.

The paper gives you three playings of every excerpt and forty minutes for the whole thing, so the playings are not three chances to catch what you missed - they are three different jobs, and doing the wrong job on the wrong playing is where marks go missing.

Part A: the four short excerpts

  • Playing 1 - answer the multiple choice and short answer questions. These ask for a fact (an element, a term, a feature) and reward speed, so get them down while the excerpt is fresh and leave the writing alone.
  • Playing 2 - annotate. Jot the elements as you hear them, in your own shorthand, against roughly where they happen. This is raw material, not sentences.
  • Playing 3 - check what you missed, and start turning your strongest annotations into the four-part response sentence from Lesson 9: name, how it is used, effect, evidence.

Part B: the six-mark question

Part B has its own three jobs, one for each step of the layer map. Before playing 1, read the question, choose your three elements and write them on your map.

  • Playing 1 - map who plays what. Fill step 1: who plays each layer, the parts, and roughly when each one starts. In the time after it, finish the labels and times.
  • Playing 2 - listen only for your three elements and fill step 2: what you hear, and in which part. In the writing time after it, write your three dot points straight from the map.
  • Playing 3 - check. Listen for the evidence in each dot point: is it where you said? Then fix your evidence and make sure every dot point has its effect.

Working rule: no full sentences while the music is playing. A sentence written while you listen is usually a description with the effect missing, because you were still listening for the next thing rather than thinking about what the last thing did.

Annotating while you listen

You cannot write full sentences while the excerpt is running, so annotation is shorthand: an element’s initial, an arrow for a change, a rough time. “D getting louder ~1:40” is a complete annotation. It is not a response sentence yet, but it has everything a response sentence needs, and that is the point of doing it in two steps rather than one.

Check yourself

Check yourself: the three quick-fire excerpts
  1. Excerpt 1, gamelan gong kebyar. The style is Balinese gamelan, from Indonesia. The two safest elements are timbre - a struck, metallic, bronze sound, with nothing bowed or blown in the whole ensemble - and texture, which is layered and interlocking, fast parts riding over slower ones rather than one tune with accompaniment underneath. Dynamics and tempo also earn the mark, because both change suddenly and the whole ensemble changes together.
  2. Excerpt 2, Vivaldi’s Spring. The style is Baroque. Timbre is a string orchestra with harpsichord underneath. The strongest second choice is structure: the opening tune keeps returning between contrasting episodes. Dynamics work too, and they are worth noticing because they are the opposite of the Grieg below - stepped between loud and soft in blocks, rather than one continuous build.
  3. Excerpt 3, Take Five. The style is jazz, and cool jazz specifically. Duration is the giveaway: five beats in the bar rather than four, which is why it feels like it is leaning or limping next to everything else you have heard this term. Timbre is alto saxophone, piano, double bass and drum kit. Structure also works - the piano pattern starts alone, the tune arrives over it, then solos run over the same repeating pattern.
  4. If you named an element but not what it was doing, that is a half-answer. "Texture" scores nothing on its own. "Layered texture, fast metal parts over slow gongs" is the answer.

Cue sheetTimed response excerpt

Unseen, and short enough to annotate start to finish. Tempo, dynamics and texture all build together here, which is exactly the kind of change the exam rewards you for catching. Play it three times, exactly as Part B runs: map first, your three elements second, check third.

  1. 0:00 into Grieg, “In the Hall of the Mountain King” (Peer Gynt)
    The ostinato theme begins low in the strings, pizzicato, very quiet and slow. Everything that happens for the next minute and a half is this same idea, gradually speeding up and growing louder.
  2. 1:00 into Grieg, “In the Hall of the Mountain King” (Peer Gynt)
    No new instrument has entered and no section has changed, but the theme is already audibly faster and louder than at 0:00. This is the trap: there is no boundary to catch here, because the composer is building continuously rather than stepping between sections. Annotate it as a gradual change, not an event.
  3. 1:42 into Grieg, “In the Hall of the Mountain King” (Peer Gynt)
    The one genuinely sudden moment in the piece: the full orchestra enters fortissimo, brass and all, at the fastest tempo yet. After ninety seconds of gradual build, this is the payoff, and it is unmistakable even on a first playing.
  4. 2:21 into Grieg, “In the Hall of the Mountain King” (Peer Gynt)
    A grand pause. The orchestra drops to complete silence for a beat, the only silence in the whole excerpt.
    1. 2:22 into Grieg, “In the Hall of the Mountain King” (Peer Gynt)
      The silence is answered immediately by the loudest, fastest chords in the piece, driving straight to the end. Silence followed by the loudest possible re-entry is itself a dynamics device, worth naming on its own.

Measured off this exact recording, not recalled: the build from 0:00 to 1:42 is a continuous rise with no internal step, which is why it is described here as gradual rather than given a false halfway boundary. The piece runs out shortly after 2:51, the last thirty seconds of the three-minute file being trailing silence.

Worked example

Question How are the elements of music used to create interest? 2 of 6 marks - one dot point of three

This models one dot point on the Grieg, using dynamics rather than Lesson 9’s timbre. The shape is identical: name, how it is used, effect, evidence. One dot point, one built sentence, all four moves inside it.

A full answer

Grieg builds the dynamics from a very quiet opening to a sudden fortissimo climax at 1:42, without ever stepping the volume up in stages, and that unbroken, gradual crescendo is what makes the eventual fortissimo feel inevitable rather than sudden: by the time the full orchestra enters, the listener has been pulled toward it for ninety seconds without a single break to relax into.

  1. Name Say which element. Dynamics.
  2. How it is used Say what you actually hear. A continuous, unbroken crescendo from very quiet to fortissimo, with no stepped stages. “It gets louder” is not enough detail on its own; continuous versus stepped is the detail that earns the mark.
  3. Effect Say what that does. It makes the climax feel inevitable and pulls the listener toward it, rather than surprising them with a sudden change.
  4. Evidence Ground it in the music. The build runs from 0:00 to the fortissimo entry at 1:42, with the grand pause at 2:21 used as a second, sharper example of the same device.
What scores nothing

“The music gets louder and louder.”

True, and it is describing, not analysing: it names the element and reports what happens, with no effect and no timed evidence attached. It is the same shape as the zero-mark timbre answer in Lesson 9.

Check yourself

Check yourself: the exam strategy
  1. In Part A, what should you write during playing 1? Nothing but the multiple choice and short answer questions. Full sentences come later.
  2. In Part B, what is playing 1 for? Mapping who plays what, on step 1 of the layer map. Your three elements get their own playing, the second one.
  3. What is an annotation, and how is it different from a response sentence? Shorthand jotted while listening - an element, a change, a rough time. A response sentence is the four-part version you write afterwards from that shorthand.
  4. Why does the Grieg excerpt’s build from 0:00 to 1:42 resist being annotated as a single boundary? Because it is continuous, not stepped - nothing new enters, the change is gradual, so marking one moment as “the change” misrepresents what is actually happening.
  5. What two things happen back to back at 2:21? A grand pause (total silence), immediately answered by the loudest, fastest chords in the piece.

How are the elements of music used to create interest? (6 marks) Three elements, one dot point each, 2 marks each. A dot point here is not a shortened paragraph and it is not a note - it is one built sentence that makes all four moves from Lesson 9: name the element, say how it is used, say what that does, give the evidence you heard. Plan first, in shorthand, straight off your annotations. The plan is where you choose your three elements, so it is the part worth two minutes.

Check yourself

Check yourself: is my response finished?
  • Three dot points, three different elements - not the same one said three ways. Dynamics building and tempo building are two elements; "it gets louder and faster" written twice is one.
  • Every dot point names an effect. A dot point that only says what happens is describing, and describing is what the zero-mark answer above does. This is the move most often missing.
  • Every dot point carries its own evidence - a time, or a clearly identified point like the entry of the full orchestra or the grand pause. The dot point has to stand up on its own, because the marker reads it on its own.
  • No dot point leans on a boundary that is not there. The build from 0:00 to 1:42 is continuous. If you have written "the music changes at 1:00", check whether anything actually changed there.
  • Each one is a built sentence, not your shorthand copied out. "D louder ~1:40" is the annotation. The dot point is what you make from it.
