Lesson 23: Comparative listening across styles

Lesson 23 of 28, in Week 9 of Sound Detectives.

Learning intention

I can compare how texture is used across three styles, using evidence from the recordings.

Success criteria

Tick them off as you go.

Cue sheetExcerpt 1: orchestra

An orchestra has dozens of players and can use any number of them at once. Follow one question the whole way through: how much of the orchestra is playing, and which part of its range?

  1. 0:04 into Gustav Holst, “Jupiter, the Bringer of Jollity”, from The Planets, Op. 32
    Busy at the top, empty underneath. Something new starts about two and a half times a second, and yet the bottom of the orchestra is silent: the two lowest bands carry under one hundredth of the sound. A texture can be crowded and thin at the same time, which is the first thing this excerpt has to teach you.
  2. 0:14 into Gustav Holst, “Jupiter, the Bringer of Jollity”, from The Planets, Op. 32
    The bottom arrives. Nothing above it changed: no new tune, no faster notes. The low register alone goes from under a fiftieth of the sound to nearly a fifth of it, and the whole thing sounds twice as heavy. Texture can thicken downwards, without a single extra note at the top.
  3. 2:36 into Gustav Holst, “Jupiter, the Bringer of Jollity”, from The Planets, Op. 32
    The orchestra shrinks to its floor. In four seconds the level falls to about a sixtieth of what it just was, and what is left is almost all low: the top two bands hold under a tenth of the sound between them, against nearly a third a moment before. Very few players, all of them at the bottom.
  4. 2:59 into Gustav Holst, “Jupiter, the Bringer of Jollity”, from The Planets, Op. 32
    The broad tune, and the most useful thirty seconds in this lesson. The orchestra is nearly as full as it gets, but the notes have almost stopped moving: through here the music starts something new well under once a second, against two to three times a second in the fast music either side. Full and busy are different things, and only one of them is texture.
  5. 4:37 into Gustav Holst, “Jupiter, the Bringer of Jollity”, from The Planets, Op. 32
    One of the loudest moments in the movement is one second behind you, and two seconds ahead the level has fallen to under a fiftieth of it. Hold this drop in mind: neither of the other two tracks goes anywhere near this quiet, and that turns out to be the biggest difference between the three.
  6. 5:01 into Gustav Holst, “Jupiter, the Bringer of Jollity”, from The Planets, Op. 32
    The fast music returns and the texture rebuilds. Over the whole movement the quiet moments sit about twenty-six times below the loud ones. That number is the one to bring to the comparison.

Nothing above names an instrument, because the measurement cannot name one: it reads register, level and how often something new starts. That is enough to describe a texture, and it is the honest limit of what was checked.

Cue sheetExcerpt 2: EDM

Nothing here is played by a room full of people. Every layer is switched on or off by one person at a desk, and you can hear the switch. Follow the bottom of the mix, the way you just did with the orchestra.

  1. 0:02 into Avicii, “Levels”
    The opening runs for over twenty seconds with no bass at all: the lowest band carries under a hundredth of the sound the whole way. It does not feel empty, because the top is full. Same trick as the orchestra’s opening, reached a completely different way.
  2. 0:23 into Avicii, “Levels”
    The drop. In two seconds the lowest band goes from under a hundredth of the sound to nearly a fifth, and over four seconds the level nearly triples. This is the same event as 0:14 in the Holst: the floor arriving. One took an orchestra and one took a fader.
  3. 0:40 into Avicii, “Levels”
    And off again. In four seconds the bass is removed, and the top carries on untouched. An orchestra cannot do this: its low players cannot stop between one bar and the next and leave everything else exactly as it was.
  4. 1:19 into Avicii, “Levels”
    The biggest single moment in the track, and it is an absence. Almost everything stops. The analysis picks this out as the strongest boundary anywhere in the three and a bit minutes, and it is made of nothing being there.
  5. 2:12 into Avicii, “Levels”
    Listen to the top disappear rather than the bottom. Over four seconds the highest band falls to almost nothing while the low-middle nearly triples: a filter closing over the whole mix. The layers are all still playing. Only their top halves have been taken away.
  6. 2:41 into Avicii, “Levels”
    The last and loudest drop. Across the whole track the quiet parts sit only about four times below the loud ones, where the orchestra managed twenty-six. Everything here lives in a narrow band of loudness, on purpose.

Cue sheetExcerpt 3: a cappella

Five singers, no instruments, no samples. Every layer in this texture is a human being who has to breathe. Follow the same question a third time: what is at the bottom, and how many parts are sounding?

  1. 0:06 into Pentatonix, “Hallelujah”
    One voice, and the video’s opening silence before it. This is the thinnest any of the three excerpts starts, and it is the only one where you can count the layers by ear from the beginning.
  2. 0:39 into Pentatonix, “Hallelujah”
    The bass voice arrives with real bottom on it: the lowest band goes from a two-hundredth of the sound to about a sixth in one second, and the level doubles. Compare that number with 0:23 in the Avicii. It is nearly the same event, and one of them is a person.
  3. 2:48 into Pentatonix, “Hallelujah”
    The bottom vanishes completely and the upper voices are left exposed. This is the strongest boundary in the track, and like the Avicii’s it is made of something leaving rather than arriving.
  4. 3:34 into Pentatonix, “Hallelujah”
    The fullest texture of the three excerpts, and the fullest thirty seconds any of them manage. Every voice is in, high in its range, and it stays there.
  5. 4:10 into Pentatonix, “Hallelujah”
    Now the voices leave from the top down. The middle band falls from a quarter of the sound to under a twentieth of it while the low band takes nearly three-fifths. The opposite of the Holst at 2:36, which shrank to its floor by losing its ceiling all at once.
  6. 4:24 into Pentatonix, “Hallelujah”
    One voice, at the very bottom. Nearly four-fifths of the sound is now below 250 Hz and almost a third of it is below 80 Hz. A texture of one, at the end of a song that spent four minutes adding parts.

The song itself finishes at about 4:29 and the level goes to zero. The video keeps running to 5:05 with different material after it, so stop the comparison at 4:29.

In classWhat we are doing

  1. Texture is the element for this lesson: how many layers are sounding, and where they sit from low to high.
  2. Work through the three excerpts in order using the timestamps. The times are clickable and jump the player.
  3. For each excerpt, write down the two moments where the texture changed most, and say what changed.
  4. Then write your comparison: one similarity and two differences, each with a time and a reason.
  5. Doing this alone? Everything you need is on this page, and the answers are at the bottom.

Your task

Compare how texture is used across the three excerpts: one similarity and two differences. Give a timestamp for every point, and say what the difference does to the listener rather than just naming it.

Check yourself

Check yourself: the comparison, worked
  1. One similarity. All three build the same way: they add the bottom last. Holst holds the low register out until 0:14, Avicii until 0:23, Pentatonix until 0:39, and in all three the arrival of the bottom is the moment the texture stops sounding light. Why it works as a similarity: it is the same musical decision reached by an orchestrator, a producer and five singers, so it says something about texture rather than about style.
  2. Difference 1: how far the texture can fall. Across the whole movement, Holst’s quiet moments sit about twenty-six times below his loud ones, and you can hear the extreme of it between 4:36 and 4:39. Across whole tracks Avicii’s sit about four times below and Pentatonix’s about four times below. What it does: the orchestra can make you lean in, and the other two never ask you to. Loudness in a modern production is held in a narrow band on purpose, so texture has to do the work that dynamics does in Holst.
  3. Difference 2: how a layer leaves. Avicii takes the bass out in four seconds at 0:40 and leaves everything above it untouched, and at 2:12 closes a filter over the whole mix so every layer loses its top half at once. Holst cannot do either: at 2:36 he also takes four seconds, but what is left is a different set of players, not the same players filtered. What it does: in the Avicii the change feels done to the music, and in the Holst it feels done by the musicians.
  4. Another difference, if you want a third. How busy the surface is. Across whole tracks, something new starts about 3.5 times a second in the Avicii, 2.8 in the Pentatonix and 1.6 in the Holst. The trap: that does not make the Holst the thinnest. At 2:59 it is running the fullest orchestra of the movement at well under one event a second. Full is about how many layers, busy is about how often they move, and they are not the same measurement.
  5. The exit question: which style used texture most surprisingly? Any of the three, if you can point at a time. The strongest answer is Pentatonix at 4:24: a song that spent four minutes stacking five voices ends on one voice, alone, with nearly four-fifths of the sound below 250 Hz. Surprising because it reverses its own method in the last ten seconds. An answer with no timestamp is not an answer.

Exit ticket

Which style used texture most surprisingly, and at what time?

Sound Detectives

Week 1: The Elements Toolkit

Welcome to our listening unit. This week we meet the six elements of music and learn to describe what we hear in clear, musical language.

  1. 1Active listening and the elements of musicI can name the elements of music and use them to describe what I hear.
  2. 2Duration and pitch (listen and play)I can identify duration and pitch features by ear.
  3. 3Writing about music 1I can write a clear sentence about how an element is used.

Week 2: Texture, Timbre and Comparing

This week we listen across styles to explore texture and timbre, and learn how to compare two works using the elements of music.

  1. 4Texture across styles (listen and build)I can identify and describe texture in different styles.
  2. 5Performing media and timbreI can identify performing media and describe timbre.
  3. 6Comparing “Valerie”I can compare two versions of the same song using performing media and timbre.

Week 3: Dynamics, Structure and Exam Writing

This week we finish the toolkit with dynamics, expression and structure, and start writing full responses to the exam question.

  1. 7Dynamics and expressionI can identify dynamics and expression and explain their effect.
  2. 8StructureI can identify the structure of a work by ear.
  3. 9Writing about music: the response frame and the exam questionI can write a full response on how the elements of music are used.

Week 4: Exam Preparation

One lesson this week, because the trial examinations compress the class to two periods and the AT3 examination follows on Wednesday of Week 5. We rehearse the whole exam in a single period: listening widely, annotating as we listen, and writing a full response under time.

  1. 10Exam preparation: wide listening, strategies and a timed responseI can use exam strategies and write to the standard of the AT3 task.

Week 5: AT3 Exam and Feedback

This week you sit the AT3 Listening Examination, then return to practical playing and review your responses to set goals.

  1. 11AT3 Listening ExaminationI can analyse how the elements of music are manipulated and combined.Assessment
  2. 12Practical recovery lessonI can contribute to a group performance with awareness.
  3. 13Exam feedback and reflectionI can evaluate my own listening response and set a goal.

Week 6: Jazz

This week we listen to jazz, play a 12-bar blues and improvise, then analyse how jazz uses the elements of music to create interest.

  1. 14Jazz listeningI can identify the features and context of jazz.
  2. 15Jazz practical: 12-bar bluesI can perform and improvise within a 12-bar blues.
  3. 16Analysing a jazz recordingI can analyse how a jazz recording’s register and dynamics change across a listening, using measured evidence.

Week 7: Film Music

This week we explore how film music creates mood and tells stories, create a short cue, and analyse a film composer’s choices.

  1. 17Film music listeningI can identify how film music supports narrative.
  2. 18Film music practical / composeI can use an element of music to create a mood for a scene.
  3. 19Analysing a film cueI can analyse how a composer builds a film cue using the elements of music.

Week 8: Music of Australia and First Nations Music

This week we listen to and analyse music of Australia, including music of Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander Peoples, with respect for context and protocols.

  1. 20Music of Australia and First Nations musicI can describe Australian music and its context respectfully.
  2. 21Australian music analysisI can analyse an Australian work using elements and context.
  3. 22Practical and response: Australian stylesI can perform a feature of an Australian style and describe it.

Week 9: Comparative and Critical Listening

This week we compare how styles use the elements differently, evaluate interpretive choices, and start thinking about the Term 4 Depth Study.

  1. 23Comparative listening across stylesI can compare how texture is used across three styles, using evidence from the recordings.
  2. 24Critical listening: evaluating choicesI can evaluate interpretive choices in performance.
  3. 25Student-choice listening and Depth Study sparkI can analyse music I choose and identify a personal area of interest.

Week 10: Synthesis and Depth Study Launch

This week we present our analyses, launch the Term 4 Depth Study, and finish with an approved proposal ready to develop.

  1. 26Synthesis presentationI can present a clear analysis of a work.
  2. 27Depth Study launch and idea generationI can generate realistic Depth Study ideas that match my interests and skills.
  3. 28Depth Study proposal (milestone) and term celebrationI can write a clear, realistic plan for my Depth Study.
