Lesson 8: Structure

Lesson 8 of 28, in Week 3 of Sound Detectives.

This is the lesson we are up to

Learning intention

I can identify the structure of a work by ear.

Success criteria

tick them off as you go, they save on your device

Key word

Structure

How the piece is put together — the order of its sections, like the shape of a story.

Read this

Structure is the only element you cannot hear in a single moment.

Every other element is there in one second of sound. You can hear that a note is high, that a chord is loud, that a tone is brassy, without waiting for anything. Structure is different. It only exists across time: it is the pattern you notice when you compare what you are hearing now with what you heard a minute ago.

What a boundary sounds like

A new section almost never announces itself with one change. It arrives with two or three at once. The usual ones:

  • instruments come in, or drop out (texture)
  • the music gets louder or softer (dynamics)
  • the drum pattern changes, or the beat drops out altogether
  • the melody changes, or an earlier melody comes back
  • the words change, or earlier words come back
  • the harmony moves somewhere it has not been

Working rule: if two or more of those change at the same moment, that moment is a boundary. If only one changes, it is usually decoration inside a section rather than the start of a new one.

You cannot be certain a section was a chorus until it comes back, which is exactly why you get three playings in a listening examination. Use the first playing to notice, and the later ones to label.

Writing it down

Use letters (A, B, A) when the sections have no names, and names (intro, verse, chorus, bridge, outro) when they do. Either way you are drawing a map: the sections in order, left to right.

Excerpt 1

OneRepublic, “Counting Stars”2013 · 4:43 · OneRepublicVEVO

A pop song with a clear verse-chorus structure. Follow one thing all the way through: the words “Lately I’ve been”. Count how many times they come back.

  1. 0:00The song opens with its chorus, over an acoustic guitar and almost nothing else. Remember this tune. You will hear it four more times.
  2. 0:21The band arrives: bass, kit, handclaps. The tune has not changed, the texture has. A boundary can be carried by texture on its own.
  3. 0:37Verse 1. New words, new melody, and the drums settle into a pattern they keep. Two changes at once, so this boundary is unmistakable.
  4. 1:18Chorus 1. The tune from 0:00, now with the full band behind it. Same tune, different dress, which is how you know 0:00 was the chorus.
  5. 3:00Bridge. Everything drops away except the voice and a few chords, and one short phrase repeats four times. This is the biggest change in the song, and the only section that happens once.

Key word

Verse-chorus

The pop-song structure: verses tell the story, the chorus is the hook that repeats.

Excerpt 2

Stevie Ray Vaughan & Double Trouble, “Pride and Joy”1983 · 3:43 · stevierayvaughnVEVO

A 12-bar blues. Nothing here is a verse or a chorus in the pop sense. The whole track is one 12-bar pattern, played through nine times without changing shape once.

  1. 0:00The riff, on guitar alone. It is the engine of the whole song and it barely stops for three and a half minutes.
  2. 0:08Bass and drums join. From here you can count: four beats to a bar, at about 127 beats a minute.
  3. 0:11The first 12 bars begin. In blues, one trip through the 12 bars is called a chorus, whether anyone sings on it or not. It is a unit of form, not a hook.
  4. 0:56Chorus 3. Follow the chords all the way through this one.
    1. 0:56Bars 1 to 4, all on the home chord (I).
    2. 1:04Bar 5. The chord steps up to IV. This is the moment that proves it is a 12-bar blues and not just a repeat.
    3. 1:08Bar 7. Back down to I.
    4. 1:11Bar 9. Up to V, the furthest from home the pattern ever goes.
    5. 1:13Bar 10. Down to IV.
    6. 1:15Bars 11 and 12. Home to I, and a turnaround that hands you back to bar 1.
  5. 1:42Chorus 5, and the guitar takes a solo. The form does not bend for it: the solo fits inside the same 12 bars as everything else.
  6. 3:35The ending. The band steps outside the pattern for the first time in the whole song, and stops on the home chord.

Each chorus runs about 22 and a half seconds. Time the gap between any two of the boundaries above and you get the same number, which is what “the form repeats unchanged” actually means when you measure it.

Key word

12-bar blues (AAB)

A repeating 12-bar chord pattern, often with an AAB lyric pattern — the backbone of blues and early rock 'n' roll.

Excerpt 3

Mozart, 12 Variations on “Ah, vous dirai-je, maman”, K. 2651781 · 14:04 · András Schiff, piano

Two forms on one track. The theme has a ternary (ABA) shape of its own, and the set then rebuilds that theme twelve times in different clothes. You already know the tune.

  1. 0:02The theme. Listen to its own shape first, before any variation starts.
    1. 0:03A. The tune, straight through.
    2. 0:10A again, note for note. Composers repeat before they contrast.
    3. 0:19B. A new middle idea, higher, stepping downwards.
    4. 0:28A returns, unchanged. A, then B, then A: that is ternary form, sitting inside the theme.
    5. 0:36B and A again, because this half of the theme is repeated too.
  2. 6:52Variation 8. The one everybody notices. Same tune, same shape, same number of bars, in a minor key. Nothing structural changed. Only the colour did.

Every variation has the same number of bars and the same chords underneath as the theme. What changes is speed, register, which hand has the fast notes, how much decoration, major or minor, and finally the metre. In other words, every element except structure. That is what a variation is.

Key word

Ternary / theme and variation

Ternary (ABA): a section, a contrasting middle, then a return to the start. Theme and variation: one melody, dressed up differently each time it repeats.

If you were away: the structure words
  • Intro - the opening section that sets the song up before the singing starts.
  • Verse - carries the story. Same tune each time, new words each time.
  • Pre-chorus - a short section between verse and chorus that builds tension and hands over.
  • Chorus - repeats with the same words and the same tune. Usually the loudest, and the one you remember.
  • Bridge - appears once, usually late, and contrasts with everything around it.
  • Instrumental (solo) - no singing, an instrument takes the lead line.
  • Outro (coda) - the ending section, after the last chorus.
  • Riff - a short pattern, usually on guitar, bass or keys, repeated as the backbone of a section.
  • Hook - the part you cannot stop singing. Often the chorus line, but it can be a riff.
  • Repetition - using the same material again. It is what makes a section recognisable when it returns.
  • Contrast - putting something different beside it. It is what makes a boundary audible in the first place.

Worked answer

Describe one thing that changes when a new section begins. (1 mark)

This is the question type your listening examination asks about structure, and it is this lesson’s exit question in the same words. Here is one full mark, on “Counting Stars”.

At 3:26 the full band returns after the bridge, so the texture thickens from one voice and a few chords to drums, bass and guitars all at once, and that thickening is what marks the start of the final chorus.

  • IdentifyName the change and say where it is. The texture thickens at 3:26. “The texture changes” on its own names an element but not a change, and earns nothing.
  • DescribeAdd the sound and the location. From one voice and a few chords to drums, bass and guitars. The marker should be able to hear it from your words without playing the track.
  • ExplainSay what it does. That thickening marks the start of the final chorus. This is the half the question is really paying for, and the half most people leave off.

What scores nothing

“A section changes at 3:26.”

True, and worth zero: it says a section changed without saying what changed. The mark is for the hearing, not for the word count, so a short answer is fine. An empty one is not.

Before you leave

What element signalled a new section most clearly?

Check yourself: four questions on today
  1. In “Counting Stars”, which section appears only once? The bridge, at 3:00. Everything else comes round at least twice.
  2. How long is one chorus of “Pride and Joy”, in seconds? About 22 and a half. Anything from 21 to 24 means you counted rather than guessed.
  3. In Mozart’s Variation 8, what changed and what stayed the same? The mode changed to minor. The tune, its length and its ABA shape stayed the same.
  4. Name two things that usually change together at a section boundary. Any two of: texture, dynamics, drum pattern, melody, words, harmony. One change on its own is usually decoration; two together is a boundary.
