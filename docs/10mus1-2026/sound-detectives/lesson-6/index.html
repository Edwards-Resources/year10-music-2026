Lesson 6: Comparing “Valerie”

Lesson 6 of 28, in Week 2 of Sound Detectives.

Learning intention

I can compare two versions of the same song using performing media and timbre.

Success criteria

tick them off as you go, they save on your device

Key word

Performing media

The voices and instruments used to perform a piece of music: in other words, what is making the sound.

Key word

Timbre (tone colour)

The quality of a sound that makes one voice or instrument sound different from another, even when they play exactly the same note. A trumpet and a flute playing the same pitch do not sound the same, and timbre is the word for that difference.

Read this

Performing media is a list. Timbre is a description. The question always wants both.

These two elements arrive together in every listening paper you will ever sit, and they ask two different things of you. Performing media is a list of what is making the sound: the voices, the instruments, how many of them. Timbre is what those sounds are actually like. You can get the list completely right and still write nothing worth a mark, because a list is not a description.

Naming what you can hear, one layer at a time

Nobody names six things at once on a first play. Take one pass per layer, in this order, and write one line after each pass:

  • The lead voice. One singer, or more than one? Sitting comfortably, or reaching?
  • Whatever carries the tune when nobody is singing. In the gaps, and in any introduction or ending.
  • Whatever holds the pulse. The layer you would tap along with. It is usually low, and usually the last thing people notice, because it is the thing you feel rather than the thing you listen to.
  • Whatever fills the middle. Chords, riffs, sustained sounds: anything sitting between the pulse and the voice.
  • Anything that happens once. A single hit, a stab, a sound that never comes back. These are what separate a good answer from a full one.

Working rule: name only what you actually heard. A guess that is wrong costs you more than a gap does, because the marker now knows you were not listening. A low sustained instrument holding the bottom is an honest answer and it earns something. An instrument that is not in the recording earns less than nothing.

Describing the colour, so somebody could hear it from your words

A timbre word is only worth writing if you can say what made you choose it. Run three tests on the sound in front of you, in any order:

  • High or low, and how much of the range does it fill? This is the test that separates rich from thin, and full from hollow.
  • Smooth or rough? Is there grit, buzz or rasp on the surface of the sound, or is it clean? This separates mellow from harsh, and polished from raw.
  • How does a note start? Struck and gone, or eased in and held? A sound with a hard edge on the front of every note reads as bright even when it is not especially high, which is the single most common reason people put the wrong word down.

Then attach the word to its source. Every timbre word needs a sound source in the same sentence. The tone colour is warm is worth nothing, because it does not say what is warm. The lead voice is husky, with a rough edge on the held notes is a description a marker can hear without playing the track, and that is the whole job.

Words that describe your reaction rather than the sound cannot be marked: nice, good, cool, catchy, sick, vibey. If a word would fit a sandwich as easily as a saxophone, leave it out.

Comparing two versions

When the tune, the words and the chords are held still, everything you can still hear is a difference in the performing.

A cover keeps the song and replaces the performance. The melody is the same, the words are the same, the chords are the same. So every difference you can hear between these two recordings is a difference in how they were performed: who is playing, what they are playing, how fast they take it, how it was recorded. That makes a pair of versions the cleanest listening test there is, because the thing you are comparing is the only thing that changed.

Writing the comparison

One similarity and one difference, and each one in three parts:

  • Name it. Which element, and which version. The 2007 version's low register is a name. It sounds different is not.
  • Give the evidence. What you heard, and when. A time makes the claim checkable, and a claim nobody can check is worth about half of one that can be.
  • Say what it does. The effect on the person listening. This is the part most people leave off, and it is usually the part carrying the second mark.

Three traps on this pair, and all three catch people

  • The second video does not start with the song. It opens with about fifty seconds of a film scene. The song itself begins at about 0:51, so every time you write for that version is counted from there. Go looking for the first verse at 0:05 and you will find dialogue.
  • The two clocks do not match. These versions run at different speeds, so the same moment of the song lands at different times on the two videos, and the gap widens as they go. Never compare 1:30 in one with 1:30 in the other. Compare events with events: the first entry of the voice against the first entry of the voice.
  • Speed is not tone colour. The difference in speed is the largest measurable difference between these two recordings and it is well worth writing about, but it belongs to duration. Label it correctly. An answer that offers a tempo difference as a difference in timbre has named the wrong element, and loses the mark it had already earned.

One last thing, and it matters for how you check your work today. Every timestamp in the two guides below was measured off these exact recordings, and a measurement can say how loud a moment is and which part of the range is carrying the sound. It cannot say what is making the sound, so neither guide names an instrument in a cue: the naming in your book is yours, built from your own ears. How you check it differs between the two versions, for a reason worth knowing before you start. One of these recordings was catalogued in full when it was released, with every player credited. The other was not. You will find out which is which, and what to do about it, once your own list is written.

Version A: the original

The Zutons, “Valerie”2006 · 3:52 · The Zutons Official

Play it once with nothing to do but notice. Then come back to the top and work the cues, clicking a time to jump the player to it. Your job on this version is the list first, then the colours: the lead voice, whatever holds the pulse, whatever fills the middle, and three tone-colour words you could defend to somebody who disagreed with you.

  1. 0:02No introduction to speak of. The level steps up here, and by 0:06 the track is at the weight it holds for the next three and a half minutes, so the whole line-up is in front of you inside six seconds. Use them: name the lead voice, then one layer underneath it, before the song has a chance to distract you.
  2. 0:10Now ask where this version puts its weight. Almost all of it is in the upper middle of the range: for practically the whole track the very bottom of the sound carries somewhere between a hundredth and a twentieth of it, while the upper middle carries between a quarter and a third. Register is not the same thing as tone colour, but it tells you where to point your ears, and up there is where this version lives. Name two things you can hear in that upper middle, and give each one a word.
  3. 1:00Count the beat. Tap along, count your taps for fifteen seconds, multiply by four, and write the number in your book. Speed is duration, not timbre, so it is not the element of today's lesson, and it is still worth thirty seconds of your time: the comparison you have to write wants a difference you can back up, and this is the one difference between these two recordings you can measure yourself with a foot and a clock. Do the same on the other version and keep both numbers.
  4. 1:40Pick any moment between here and the end, and it is at much the same weight as 0:06 was. Across three and a half minutes the level never leaves a narrow band, the harmony turns a corner only six times in the whole song, and frame to frame the sound changes about half as much as the other version's does. So the line-up you wrote down at the start is very likely still the line-up you are hearing now. Check that by ear, because the measurement cannot check it for you: all it can say is that if anything left, it left quietly.
  5. 2:32The one place in this track where the band gets out of the way, and the best fifteen seconds on the page for hearing a tone colour on its own. Over the next ten seconds the level falls to under a third of what it is right now, the bottom of the sound very nearly disappears, and the top of it climbs. Fewer things sounding means each one is easier to name and easier to describe, so this is where you collect your colour words for this version.
    1. 2:34The first step down, and it is a step rather than a fade: about three quarters of what it was, arriving all at once.
    2. 2:36Four seconds in and the floor has gone: the low band is down to about a sixth of what it held at 2:32. Whatever was holding the bottom of this track has stopped, which means you can now hear what had been sitting on top of it.
    3. 2:41The quietest the track gets between its start and its ending: under a third of the level it ran at ten seconds ago. The energy has moved to the top of the range as well, where the highest band has roughly tripled since 2:32. Low sounds gone, high ones left. Two colour words minimum before you move on.
  6. 2:45And back, about half again as loud in a single step. Now listen to what does not come back with it: the bottom of the sound is still nearly as empty here as it was in the dip, and it does not return to the weight it had before until about 2:54, eight or nine seconds later. The level and the low end come back separately, and hearing that gap is what separates a real answer from a guess. So the shape to remember on this version is out of the way, then back in stages, about twenty seconds from 2:34 to 2:54. The other version does nothing like it.

Not one cue above names an instrument, and that is deliberate: what was measured is level and register, not who is playing. This recording's own release credits carry the same gap. Checked against the catalogue, they name a producer and two engineers and not one performer, so nobody ever published a list to check this version's naming against. That is what the label wrote down, not a shortcut this page is taking. Your list here stands on your own ears alone, and that makes it real work rather than a lesser answer.

Version B: the cover

Mark Ronson featuring Amy Winehouse, “Valerie”2007 · 4:40 · Mark Ronson

Do not press play and start writing. This video opens with about fifty seconds of a film scene, and the song does not begin until about 0:51. Every time you write for this version counts from there. Work the cues in order, then do the same two jobs you did on the original: the list, then the colours. Once your own list is written, there is a real answer key for this version further down the page. Keep it closed until then.

  1. 0:24Still not the song. Listen to what this sound is made of: there is effectively nothing in the bottom of the range, somewhere between a five-hundredth and a hundredth of it, and everything is stacked in the middle and upper middle. That is what talking measures like, and it is worth hearing, because it is the exact opposite of what arrives at 0:51. Write nothing down yet.
  2. 0:42The scene runs out. Across these few seconds what is left falls to under a third of itself and then holds at almost nothing. If you ever lose your place on this video, find this gap: the song is on the other side of it.
  3. 0:51The song. This is the largest single step anywhere in the file, the level up by more than two and a half times in one moment, and the harmony turns over completely at the same instant. Listen to where the new sound sits: about a fifth of everything you can now hear is in the very bottom of the range, where a second ago there was nothing at all. Something low has arrived. Name it if you can hear what it is, describe where it sits if you cannot, and from here on treat 0:51 as this version's zero.
    1. 0:54Three seconds later there is more down there, not less: nearly a quarter of the whole sound is in the bottom band. This opening stretch has more weight underneath it than anywhere else on either version, so it is the easiest place on the page to hear what separates the two recordings.
  4. 1:00Count the beat here, exactly as you did on the original. Do it before you look at the number you wrote over there, so that you are not talking yourself into an answer. The two numbers sit further apart than almost anybody guesses, and that gap is the one difference between these recordings you can prove without naming a single instrument.
  5. 1:36Stop naming things for a moment and listen to how fast the surface of this music moves. Frame to frame it changes about twice as much as the original does, and across its length it turns three times as many harmonic corners. That is a measurement of activity, not of how many instruments are playing, so do not write it up as a texture answer. What it means for you is practical: colours arrive and leave faster here, so you need shorter listening windows to catch them than you needed on the original.
  6. 2:01A step down, to about three quarters, and it holds rather than bouncing straight back. This is the one sustained drop inside the song, and a drop is your best chance at an individual colour, because there is less piled on top of it. Listen twice: once to decide whether anything actually left, and once to decide whether everything simply got quieter. Those are different events, and only your ears can tell them apart.
  7. 3:02And a step back up, about a quarter louder, held. Those are this version's two big level moves, and they sit a full minute apart. The original does something that looks similar on paper and is not: its drop and its recovery are ten seconds apart. Same pair of moves, entirely different shape. Writing that the two versions handle their quiet moment the same way is the easiest wrong answer available today.
  8. 4:10Full weight here, and about fourteen seconds of song left. Stop your comparison at 4:24: from there the bottom of the sound goes to nothing and the level falls away, and what runs on to the end of the file is not the track. Anything you write about how this version ends has to be about what happens before 4:24.

Every time above is this video's own clock. This version runs faster than the original, so nothing here has a matching timestamp over there, and a comparison written by lining the two clocks up will be wrong on every line. If you were away: the twelve timbre words
  • Bright - plenty of energy at the top of the sound, with a hard edge on the front of each note. It cuts through whatever else is playing. A trumpet high in its range, or a cymbal struck hard.
  • Dark - the opposite: the top is missing or covered over, so the sound feels closed in. Not the same as quiet, and not the same as low. A clarinet at the bottom of its range, or a piano played with the soft pedal down.
  • Warm - full in the lower middle, smooth on the surface, no hard edges anywhere. The word most people reach for when a sound is comfortable to sit inside. A cello in its middle register, or an electric piano played gently.
  • Mellow - soft-edged and even, with nothing sticking out of it. A warm sound played gently: a flugelhorn, or a nylon-string guitar picked with the fingers.
  • Harsh - rough and hard at once, with an edge that grates rather than excites. Worth saying when it is deliberate, and it often is. An electric guitar with the distortion up, or a saxophone pushed hard at the top of its range.
  • Rich - a lot going on inside the one note, high and low together, so a single sound seems to fill the range on its own. A pipe organ with several stops pulled out, or a grand piano with the sustain pedal down.
  • Thin - the opposite of rich. A narrow sound with nothing underneath it and not much around it. A recorder, or one voice singing alone.
  • Hollow - the middle of the sound is missing while the outsides are still there. It sounds like something with a hole in it, because usually it is. Blowing across the top of a bottle, or a marimba bar struck softly.
  • Husky - a voice with air and grain in it, slightly rough, a little worn. A word for voices far more often than for instruments, and it fits a singer working near the bottom of their range.
  • Breathy - you can hear the air moving as well as the note. Common in a quiet voice, or a flute low in its range.
  • Nasal - pinched and narrow, as though the sound is coming out through a small opening. It describes a shape, not a fault. An oboe, or a harmonica.
  • Raw - unpolished on purpose: rough edges left in and nothing smoothed off. The word for a recording that sounds like a room rather than a studio, or a voice recorded with nothing done to it afterwards.

Your task

Write one similarity and one difference between the two versions, each backed with musical evidence and its effect. Performing media and timbre are marked together as one 2-mark question in the listening exam: naming the instruments alone only gets you one mark, you need the tone-colour description too.

Worked answer

Describe one difference between these two versions of “Valerie”, using musical evidence. (2 marks)

This is the shape of the comparison you are writing today, and the shape of the comparison question in a listening paper. Here is one full answer, built on something you can hear rather than something you assumed.

The 2007 version carries far more of its sound low in the range than the 2006 one does: from the moment the song starts at 0:51, about a fifth of everything you can hear is in the very bottom of the sound, where the original keeps its weight up in the upper middle for the whole three and a half minutes, so the newer version feels heavy and pushed along from underneath while the original sounds lighter and thinner under the voice.

  • IdentifyName the difference and say which version it belongs to. The 2007 version has much more low sound in it. If you can hear what is making that low sound, name the instrument instead, because naming is what performing media is for. Name only what you actually heard: a wrong name costs you more than a gap does. They sound different names nothing and earns nothing.
  • DescribeGive the evidence, and put a time on it. From 0:51, about a fifth of the sound is right at the bottom of the range, and the original keeps almost nothing down there at any point. A time makes the claim checkable, and a marker should be able to hear your answer without playing either track.
  • ExplainSay what the difference does to the music. The newer version feels heavy and pushed from underneath, and the original sounds lighter. This is what the second mark is for, and it is the half most people leave off.

What scores nothing

“The Amy Winehouse version is heaps better, it has way more soul and the instruments sound amazing.”

Three claims and not one of them is about a sound. Better is a preference, soul is a genre word standing in for a description, and the instruments sound amazing names no instrument, offers no colour word and points at no moment in either recording. There is no time anywhere in it, so there is nothing a marker could check. A short answer would be fine here. An empty one is not.

Before you leave

Which version's tone colour suits the song better, and why? Name at least one voice or instrument in your answer.

Check yourself: five questions on today
  1. You counted the beat on both versions. What should the two numbers be, and which element is that? About 83 beats a minute on the 2006 version and about 108 on the 2007 one, which puts the newer version roughly 29 per cent ahead. Anything within about five either way means you counted rather than guessed. The element is duration, so it goes into your comparison as a difference in speed and nothing else.
  2. Which version puts more of its sound at the bottom of the range, and where is that easiest to hear? The 2007 one, and the clearest case is the stretch straight after 0:51, where roughly a fifth to a quarter of everything sounding is in the lowest band. On the 2006 version that band holds between a hundredth and a twentieth for practically the whole track, which is why it reads as lighter even when it is just as loud.
  3. Where does each version get out of the way, and why does that matter today? The 2006 version at 2:34, where the level drops to under a third and the floor of the sound nearly vanishes for about ten seconds. The 2007 version never thins out that far: its one sustained drop, at 2:01, only takes it to about three quarters. It matters because a thinner moment is where a single colour can be heard on its own, so those are the seconds where the naming actually gets done.
  4. One similarity worth writing, in all three parts. Name it: both versions are the same song underneath, the same melody, the same words and the same chords, each carried by one lead voice with a group behind it. Evidence: the tune you can sing over one is the tune you can sing over the other, and in both of them that tune sits in a single voice rather than being shared around. Effect: because the song itself is held still, everything you can hear that differs is down to the performing, which is how two recordings of one song end up feeling like two different pieces of music to whoever is listening. The melody, words and chords you can take as given, because that is what makes the 2007 recording a cover. The one-voice-and-a-group half you check the way you check every name on this page today: against the 2007 credit list where one exists, and by ear where it does not.
  5. The exit question: which version's tone colour suits the song better? Either version can be defended, and the mark is in three moves rather than in the choice: name a source you actually heard, give it a word from the bank, and say what that word does to the song. Written out it is one sentence, with the brackets filled from your own listening: The 2007 version suits it better, because the (source you named) sounds (your word from the bank), and that (what the word does) suits a song that (what the song is doing there). Filled in, it looks like this. Say the word you would defend for the lead voice on the 2007 version is husky: then your sentence runs The 2007 version suits it better, because the lead voice is husky, with a grain on it that a smoother voice would not have, and that roughness makes the song sound like somebody telling you something rather than performing it. That is a full answer if husky is genuinely your word and you can point at the moment that made you choose it. It is worth nothing if you copied it off this page, and a marker can tell, because the next question will ask you where you heard it. Two answers that do not get there at all: The 2007 one, because it sounds better, which makes none of the three moves, and The 2007 one, because it has more low sound in it, which says where the sound sits rather than what it is like. Register points your ears at the right place. It is not a tone colour, and it will not do the job of one.
