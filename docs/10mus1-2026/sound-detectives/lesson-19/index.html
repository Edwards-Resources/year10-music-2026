Lesson 19: Analysing a film cue

Lesson 19 of 28, in Week 7 of Sound Detectives.

Learning intention

I can analyse how a composer builds a film cue using the elements of music.

Success criteria

Tick them off as you go.

Learn

A cue does not need separate sections to have a shape. It can build in a single continuous rise instead.

Every excerpt so far this term has moved in sections you could label: a verse handing over to a chorus, a 12-bar cycle repeating, a theme returning after its variations. A film cue does not have to work that way. It can be built as one continuous move in a single direction, with nothing holding it together except the level rising.

Testing for a single build

You do not have to trust your ear alone for this. Use the volume knob in your head, the one lesson 1 gave you, and take a reading off it every fifteen to twenty seconds from somewhere near the start to somewhere near the end, and write down whether each one is louder than the last, softer, or about the same.

Working rule: if every reading breaks the highest one so far, and nothing sustained ever brings the level back down, the whole cue is one build, not a set of sections. One step down inside a build does not undo it, as long as the very next reading clears the record again.

Key word

Cue
A continuous stretch of film music, timed and shaped as a single unit. This lesson analyses one cue as a whole, not as a set of contrasting sections.

Learn

The loudest moment and the biggest single move are not the same thing, and an ending can be a cut rather than a fade.

Reading where an arrival lands

A big move inside a build is a jump you can time to under a second, and where it lands in the range matters as much as how big it is. An arrival that lands in the bottom of the range reads as large and heavy. One that lands in the top reads as bright and thin. You can hear and describe that difference without naming a single instrument: it is a question of register, not of what is making the sound.

Stopping is not the same as fading

A cue can end two ways. A fade is even: everything falls away together, at the same rate. A cut is not. The playing simply stops, and what is left afterwards is only whatever the room does to the sound before it dies out.

Working rule: check the top of the range and the bottom of the range separately in the seconds after the loudest moment. If the top empties out fast while the bottom holds on and keeps climbing a little longer, that gap is the room ringing on a low sound after the top has already gone quiet. It is one ending, not two.

Cue sheet

Play it once, start to finish, before you open a single cue below. This cue has no separate sections to label: it is one continuous rise from a quiet, narrow opening to a loud, wide peak, then a sudden stop. The two elements you are analysing today are how loud it gets and how wide the sound becomes. Then come back to the top and work through the cues below in order.

  1. 0:05 into Hans Zimmer, “Cornfield Chase” (Interstellar)
    The cue opens quiet, and it stays quiet for a while: it drifts a little lower over the next twenty seconds before it starts to climb. This is your starting point for the build test set out above, so fix it in your ear now.
  2. 0:25 into Hans Zimmer, “Cornfield Chase” (Interstellar)
    This is the quietest point in the whole cue, quieter even than the opening. From here the level goes up more than twenty times to the peak at 1:58, and the only sustained step down on the way is the one at 0:44, which you will hear in a moment and which is not what it sounds like.
  3. 0:35 into Hans Zimmer, “Cornfield Chase” (Interstellar)
    For several seconds here the sound narrows into one region of the range: two neighbouring parts of it carry nearly nine tenths of everything sounding, and all the rest of the range together is under a tenth. Nothing underneath, almost nothing on top. Hold that thinness in your ear, because of what happens next.
    1. 0:42 into Hans Zimmer, “Cornfield Chase” (Interstellar)
      The single biggest event in the whole cue. In one second the level roughly triples, and the bottom of the range more than triples with it: something large arrives, and it arrives low, under that thin, narrow sound.
    2. 0:44 into Hans Zimmer, “Cornfield Chase” (Interstellar)
      The one real dip in the whole build, and it is not a fall so much as a settle: the level eases back to about three fifths of what it just reached, then the climb picks up again. A dip straight after the biggest arrival in the piece does not break the build.
  4. 1:00 into Hans Zimmer, “Cornfield Chase” (Interstellar)
    Check the top of the range right now, at any point you like: there is almost nothing there, and there has not been since the start. Whatever fills this cue out, none of it lives at the top, and none of it will for another minute, right up to the peak.
  5. 1:23 into Hans Zimmer, “Cornfield Chase” (Interstellar)
    About half again as loud across two seconds, and the bottom of the range arrives with it for the second time: it goes up around eight times over, a bigger jump in the bottom than the arrival at 0:42 managed. The same move, made again and harder, on top of everything already there. That is the last gear before the peak.
  6. 1:58 into Hans Zimmer, “Cornfield Chase” (Interstellar)
    1:58 is the loudest the cue gets,. It is also the widest the range has been: compared with 0:35, the sound has spread out of that one narrow strip into the bottom, the middle and the upper part of the range together. Loud, busy and wide, all at once, which is where this build finally lands.
    1. 1:59 into Hans Zimmer, “Cornfield Chase” (Interstellar)
      One second on, the overall level has already dropped by about a fifth, but the bottom of the range is doing the opposite: it has gone up, not down. The top and bottom have stopped moving together.
    2. 2:00 into Hans Zimmer, “Cornfield Chase” (Interstellar)
      By 2:00 the overall level has fallen to about a quarter of the peak, but the bottom is still climbing, and by now it is several times what it read at the peak second. The top of the sound is going. The bottom is not.
    3. 2:01 into Hans Zimmer, “Cornfield Chase” (Interstellar)
      The last second this page can show you. The top of the range has all but gone, and the bottom is carrying more of the sound than at any point since the arrival at 0:42. That is what a cut sounds like rather than a fade: the playing stops, and what is left is the room ringing on the lowest part of the sound after everything above it has already died away.

Worked example

Question Explain how the composer builds intensity across this cue, using two elements of music. 3 marks

This is the shape of the response task on this page, asking about two elements rather than one. Here is one full answer, built from the arrival at 0:42 and the loudness build either side of it.

A full answer

At 0:42 the level jumps by about three and a half times in a single second and the bottom of the range more than triples with it, so the arrival lands as something large and low rather than as more of the same sound getting bigger. That arrival sits inside a longer climb: from 0:25 the level rises more than twenty times to 1:58, and the one step down on the way, at 0:44, is the settling after that arrival rather than the start of a new section, so the whole cue reads as one continuous build rather than a set of contrasting sections. Dynamics and texture are doing this together, not one after the other.

  1. Identify Name both elements and where the key moment is. Dynamics and texture, at 0:42. Naming only one of the two loses a mark on a two-element question.
  2. Describe Say what changes and how far, for both elements. The level roughly triples, and the bottom of the range more than triples with it. A marker should be able to hear it from your words.
  3. Explain Say what the two elements do together, not separately. The arrival lands as something large and low, inside a build that never lets up for ninety seconds. This is the mark almost everyone leaves off, because it means connecting the two elements rather than describing them side by side.
What scores nothing

“The dynamics and texture in this cue are very dramatic and build up the tension really well.”

Names both elements and says they are good, which is true of almost any effective cue and proves nothing about this one. No time, no direction, no evidence. A short answer earns marks. An empty one does not.

In classWhat we are doing

  1. Read the explain blocks: how to test for a single build, how to read an arrival, and how a cut differs from a fade.
  2. Listen to the cue once straight through, no cues open.
  3. Work through the cues in order, tracking the build and the arrival at 0:42.
  4. Write a response using two elements: dynamics and texture.
  5. Check your answer against the worked example and the self-check.

Your task

Write a response: explain how the composer builds intensity across this cue, using dynamics and texture as your two elements. Use at least two moments from the cues above as evidence.

Exit ticket

Name one moment from today’s cue, and the two elements it shows changing together.

Check yourself

Check yourself: four questions on today
  1. Between 0:25 and the peak at 1:58, does the loudness ever take a sustained step backward? Only once, at 0:44, and that follows the cue’s biggest arrival rather than breaking the build.
  2. What part of the range does the arrival at 0:42 land in? The bottom of the range, not the top.
  3. For most of the two minutes before the ending, what is happening at the top of the range? Next to nothing. It stays close to empty the whole way through, only moving once the cue is ending.
  4. At the very end, do the top and bottom of the range fall away at the same rate? No. The top empties out fast while the bottom keeps climbing for a second or two longer, which is what a cut sounds like rather than a fade.

Slides and notes

  • A subwoofer or decent headphones helps for the ending: the bottom ringing on at 2:00 to 2:01 is easy to miss on laptop speakers.
