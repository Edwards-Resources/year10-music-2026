Lesson 5: Performing media and timbre

Lesson 5 of 28, in Week 2 of Sound Detectives.

Learning intention

I can identify performing media and describe timbre.

Success criteria

tick them off as you go, they save on your device

Key word

Tone colour

What's making the sound, and what it actually sounds like: an instrument's ‘personality’.

Naming what you hear

Sort by family first. The name comes after, not before.

You already know more instrument families than you think you do, and you use them without noticing every time you separate one sound from another inside a wall of noise. The problem is never that you cannot hear a separate sound. It is that you reach straight for a name - guitar, drums, whatever the style usually has - before you have actually listened to how the sound is being made. Sort first, name second, and most of the guessing disappears.

The sorting questions

  • Voice or instrument? A voice has words, or at least a vowel shape, even when it is wordless.
  • If it is an instrument, how is the sound started? Plucked or struck (a sharp attack, then the note decays away on its own), bowed or blown (the player can hold the note as long as they like, or make it swell), or electronic (a sound with no obvious physical cause at all - no breath, no string, no skin).
  • Does the note ring on after it starts, or stop the instant the player stops? A struck bell rings on its own; a drum does not.
  • Is there one source, or several doing the same job at once? A section of violins moving together sounds different from one violin alone, even before you know the word ‘section’.

Working rule: answer those four questions before you reach for a name. ‘Plucked, decays fast, one source’ gets you to guitar or harp long before ‘sounds like a guitar’ does, and it also gets you somewhere useful when the actual instrument is one you have never heard of. You do not need the exact name to earn the mark. You need a description precise enough that somebody else could not mistake it for a different family.

When you genuinely cannot name it

That happens, and it does not have to cost you anything. ‘A struck metal instrument, not a drum’ is a real, defensible answer even if you never learn the exact word for it. What earns nothing is skipping the sorting altogether and writing down whatever instrument the style usually has.

Describing tone colour

A timbre word has to survive being written down without the music playing.

‘It sounds nice’ and ‘it sounds warm’ are both one word longer than nothing, but only one of them is a timbre answer. The difference is what the word is describing. ‘Nice’ describes how the sound makes you feel. ‘Warm’ describes a property of the sound itself, and it would still be true of that recording next week, played to somebody in a completely different mood.

The test

Ask whether the word could just as easily describe a surface, a light or a temperature as it describes a sound: bright, dark, warm, cold, thin, rich, harsh, smooth, rough, hollow, buzzy, breathy, reedy, metallic, nasal. All of those pass, because they are borrowed from touch and light on purpose - tone colour is a metaphor, and English's whole timbre vocabulary leans on it. ‘Nice’, ‘cool’, ‘sick’, ‘good’ all fail, because none of them describe anything about the sound. They describe you.

Working rule: if the word could just as easily describe a jumper or a photograph, it is a timbre word. If it only says how you rate the sound, it is not.

Where the words come from

You do not need to invent a word on the spot. Register gives you a head start: a sound sitting high in the range, with most of its energy well above the middle, tends to earn ‘bright’ or ‘thin’; one sitting low and full tends to earn ‘warm’ or ‘rich’. That is a starting point, not a rule you can apply blind - the same register can sound harsh or smooth depending on how the note is started - but it is a faster way in than guessing from nothing.

Listen: solo sitar

Sahana Banerjee, “Endearing Raag Piloo”6:33 · darbarfestival

One performer, one instrument, six and a half minutes, recorded at the Darbar festival. Nothing to hide behind and nowhere for a second sound to come from: the hardest possible test of naming and describing, because there is only ever one thing to listen to.

  1. 0:00A brief low thump right at the start. That is the recording beginning, not the performance: it is gone within five seconds and never returns. Do not count it as an instrument entering.
  2. 0:05From here to the very end, listen for what never arrives: almost nothing sounds below 250 Hz on this recording, about one part in a hundred of everything you hear. The middle of the range carries about four fifths of everything you hear, packed into two adjacent bands. A range that narrow is what one sustained source sounds like on its own; more sources than that usually reach wider than this.
  3. 6:25Six and a half minutes in, and the bottom of the range is exactly as empty as it was at 0:05: still down around one part in a hundred. Nothing new has ever stepped in underneath this performance. On a page about naming what is making a sound, the honest answer for the register under 250 Hz here is: almost nothing, the whole way through.

Listen: voice and strings

Louis Armstrong, “What A Wonderful World”2:19 · Louis Armstrong

A solo voice against a string section, with no trumpet or rhythm section audible anywhere on this recording. You meet this same track again as the worked example further down this page, so listen for the media and timbre now and there is less to do later.

  1. 0:02The first step: loudness jumps by about three fifths in a single second, as the first sound source arrives.
  2. 0:06A second step, about half again as loud as the moment before. Something else has just joined what was already sounding.
  3. 0:14A third step, and after this one nothing else arrives or drops out for the rest of the track. Whatever is sounding by here is the whole performing ensemble, for the next two minutes.
  4. 1:00Across the body of the track, the middle of the range carries about three fifths of the sound's energy between two adjacent bands, with about a quarter reaching above 2500 Hz. A texture that sits in the middle rather than reaching for brightness: match that to what is carrying the tune and what is sitting underneath it.
  5. 2:03The single biggest harmonic change on the whole track happens here, and it is quieter than the body around it, not louder: a change carried by less of the sound than usual. With the ensemble pulled back like this, one of the two sources you have been listening for should be more exposed than the other. Listen for which one is carrying that change alone.

Listen: full orchestra

John Williams, “Main Title”5:24 · John Williams

The recording's own title is only “Main Title”, with no film name or year on record; the channel credited is John Williams. Nearly five and a half minutes, built around two huge arrivals rather than one - the opposite shape to the voice and the sitar you have just heard.

  1. 1:28Before the first big arrival, this track is nearly silent: barely above a whisper, and almost all of what little is sounding sits in the middle of the range.
    1. 1:32The arrival begins: loudness starts climbing out of that near-silence.
    2. 1:36The bottom of the range arrives with it: a band that was barely present a moment ago now carries about eight times what it did, in the same stretch the loudness keeps climbing.
    3. 1:40By here the arrival is complete: loudness has multiplied more than eleven times across the whole ten seconds from 1:28, which is the biggest arrival anywhere in this recording. Two different things are being measured when you compare arrivals: how far the level travels across the whole arrival, and how big its single largest step is. This one doubles in its largest step, like the two that follow, and the eleven times is the whole journey out of near-silence. A change this large, spread across this much of the range, is the shape a full ensemble makes when it comes in together, not one instrument.
  2. 3:12A second arrival, and it is about the same size as the first: the level roughly doubles again in one step, just as it did at 1:34. The same kind of jump as before, not just a louder moment - when a change this size repeats, it usually means the same forces coming back rather than new ones arriving.
  3. 4:37A third arrival, but a smaller one this time: up by about two fifths, against the doubling of the first two. A jump this size is consistent with fewer performing forces returning, or the same ones re-entering more quietly, rather than an arrival on the scale of 1:40 or 3:12. Listen for whether this sounds like the same forces as before or something smaller.
  4. 4:55Now the bottom disappears again while the top does not: almost nothing below 250 Hz, while the middle and upper-middle of the range are still sounding clearly. Whatever is carrying the tune up there has been left on its own.
  5. 5:13The ending is a fall in two steps, not a fade: down to about a third of what it was, then down again to a little over a third of that, in the space of four seconds. That is a texture cut in blocks, the kind of ending you get from an ensemble stopping together, not one instrument trailing off on its own.

Set the three side by side, because this is the one thing on this page you can count rather than guess. The sitar puts about four fifths of its energy into two neighbouring middle bands, with about one part in a hundred below 250 Hz. Armstrong spreads wider: about three fifths through the middle, about a seventh below 250 Hz and about a quarter above 2500 Hz. The orchestra spreads widest of the three, a little over half through the middle. That spread is not a coincidence of the recording. It is what more sound sources playing at once does to the range, and it is why the three sound as far apart as they do before you have named a single instrument.

If you were away: performing media words
  • Solo - one performer, alone.
  • Duet - two performers.
  • Trio - three performers.
  • Quartet - four performers.
  • Ensemble - any group playing or singing together; the general word when nothing more specific fits.
  • Orchestra - a large mixed ensemble built from string, woodwind, brass and percussion sections together.
  • Choir - a group of singers, usually more than one voice on each part.
  • A cappella - voices alone, with no instruments at all.
  • Rhythm section - the instruments holding the beat and the harmony underneath everything else: usually some mix of drums, bass, guitar and keyboard.
If you were away: instrument families
  • Strings - any instrument that makes its sound from a vibrating string, plucked, bowed or struck: violin, cello, guitar, sitar, harp.
  • Woodwind - a column of air set vibrating by breath across or through a reed or an edge: flute, clarinet, oboe, saxophone.
  • Brass - a column of air set vibrating by the player's own lips against a mouthpiece: trumpet, trombone, French horn, tuba.
  • Percussion - struck, shaken or scraped: drum kit, timpani, tambourine, cymbals.
  • Keyboard - played with keys, however the sound is actually produced underneath them: piano, organ, synthesiser.
  • Electronic - a sound with no obvious physical cause: synthesiser, drum machine, an electric instrument run through an effect.
  • Voice - the human voice itself, described by range or style rather than an instrument name: soprano, tenor, gravelly, spoken.
  • None of today's three recordings has a keyboard or an electronic instrument in it: the sitar excerpt is one string instrument alone, the Armstrong excerpt is voice and strings only, and the orchestra draws on strings, woodwind, brass and percussion, which is what ‘full orchestra’ means. Those two rows are legitimately blank today, not something you missed.
  • This table has no single fixed answer, because it asks what you heard, not what is always true. The right answer is the real instrument you can point to in one of today's three recordings and defend if somebody disagrees with you.

Your task

Worked answer

Identify the performing media in this excerpt and describe its timbre. (2 marks - one for performing media, one for timbre)

This is the shape of the actual exam question: performing media and timbre are worth two marks together, one for naming what you hear and one for describing it. Here is one full answer, on the Armstrong recording above.

This is a solo voice set against a string section, with no other instrument audible anywhere in the recording: a warm, breathy vocal tone over a smooth, sustained string tone underneath it.

  • IdentifyName what is making the sound, precisely enough that somebody else could recognise the same recording from your words. A solo voice, with a string section behind it and nothing else audible. Getting the family right earns the mark, even without naming every player in the section.
  • DescribeGive a real timbre word for what you named, not a vague one. Warm and breathy for the voice, smooth and sustained for the strings. Both words would survive being written down without the recording playing, which is the test from earlier on this page.

What scores nothing

“It sounds beautiful and really sets the mood.”

A taste judgement, not a listening answer. It never says what is making the sound, so the first mark is not there, and ‘beautiful’ is not a tone colour word: it says how the answer-writer feels, not what the recording sounds like, so the second mark is not there either.

If you were away: the other two excerpts
  • Sitar excerpt - performing media: a solo sitar, one instrument playing alone with nothing else audible. Timbre: a buzzy, resonant plucked-string tone with a steady drone underneath it - a sound that almost never reaches down into the bottom of the range, about one part in a hundred of what you hear, which is part of what makes it feel so exposed.
  • Orchestra excerpt - performing media: a full orchestra, strings, woodwind, brass and percussion all contributing rather than one instrument on its own. Timbre: rich and full when the whole ensemble is playing together, thinning to a brighter, less full sound at the moments when only the middle and upper parts of the range are left sounding.

Before you leave

Pick one instrument and describe its timbre in a full sentence.

Check yourself: four questions on today
  1. What is the difference between performing media and timbre? Performing media is what is making the sound - the voices and instruments. Timbre is what that sound is actually like.
  2. How much of the sitar recording's sound sits below 250 Hz, from 0:05 to the very end? Almost nothing - about one part in a hundred of everything you hear, the whole six and a half minutes.
  3. On the Armstrong recording, when does the ensemble finish arriving? By about 0:15. Nothing steps in or drops out again for the rest of the track.
  4. What is the test for whether a timbre word is actually doing its job? It has to describe the sound the way you would describe a surface or a light: warm, bright, harsh, thin. If it only says how the sound made you feel, it fails the test.
