Lesson 9: Writing about music: the response frame and the exam question

Lesson 9 of 28, in Week 3 of Sound Detectives.

Learning intention

I can write a full response on how the elements of music are used.

Success criteria

tick them off as you go, they save on your device

Key word

The response sentence

Every point you write about music has four parts: name the element, say how it is used, explain its effect, and give the musical evidence you actually heard. Miss the effect and you are describing, not analysing. One of these sentences is one dot point, and a full response is three of them.

Read this

Every mark in this exam is earned by the word after “because”.

A response sentence has four parts: name the element, say how it is used, say what effect that has, and give the musical evidence you actually heard. Miss the effect and you have described the music, not analysed it. Description on its own earns nothing in this exam.

The four parts

  • Name - which element you are writing about (dynamics, structure, timbre, and so on).
  • How it is used - what you actually hear, in enough detail that the marker can hear it too.
  • Effect - what that does: to the mood, to the listener, to the piece. This is the “because”.
  • Evidence - where in the music it happens. A timestamp is evidence. So is “throughout”, if it is true.

Working rule: if your sentence would still be true with the effect deleted, you have not written the effect. “The dynamics get louder” survives without a because. “The dynamics get louder, which builds tension toward the chorus” does not. Delete the second half and the sentence falls over. That is the test.

Describing vs analysing

  • Describing (no marks): “The orchestra plays softly under the voice.” True, and it stops at naming what happens.
  • Analysing (full marks): “The orchestra plays softly under the voice, which lets the rough, worn quality of the voice come through instead of being covered by the sound around it.” Same observation, with the because attached.

The exam question is always some version of how are the elements of music used to create interest? Part B is worth 6 of the 20 marks, so a full response covers three elements, one dot point like the ones above for each. Three dot points, not three paragraphs: each one is a single built sentence that stands up on its own.

Listen

A work we have already analysed, so that the writing is the new part, not the listening: Louis Armstrong, 'What a Wonderful World'.

Worked answer

How are the elements of music used to create interest? (2 of 6 marks - one dot point of three)

This models one of your three dot points: timbre, on “What a Wonderful World”. The other two are yours to write, the same four-part shape, applied to whatever you actually hear.

Armstrong sings the whole track in his rough, gravelly voice, set against a smooth orchestra of strings, and that contrast in tone colour is what gives the song its warmth: a worn, human voice sitting over something polished and safe makes the song feel spoken to you rather than performed at you.

  • NameSay which element. Timbre. Naming it and stopping is where the zero answer below goes wrong.
  • How it is usedSay what you actually hear, in contrast. Armstrong’s rough, gravelly voice against a smooth string orchestra. Two tone colours named against each other, not one word like “orchestra” on its own.
  • EffectSay what that contrast does. It makes the song feel warm and personal, like it is being spoken rather than performed. This is the half of the sentence most people leave off, and the half that earns the mark.
  • EvidenceGround it in the music. Throughout the track, and the voice never changes character against the strings. Evidence does not have to be a timestamp. Something being true for the whole piece is evidence too.

What scores nothing

“The song uses timbre.”

True, and worth nothing: it names the element and stops. No sound has been described, no effect has been claimed, nothing has been heard. It is the same shape as the zero-mark answer to structure in Lesson 8: naming an element is not analysing it.

Check yourself: the response sentence
  1. Which part of the response sentence is most often left out? The effect. A sentence that only says what happens is describing, not analysing.
  2. Is “the song uses dynamics” worth any marks on its own? No. It names the element and stops, with no description, no effect, no evidence.
  3. What is the working rule for spotting a missing effect? Delete the second half of the sentence. If it still stands up on its own, the effect was never there.
  4. How many elements does a full response need, going by this lesson’s exam question? Three - one dot point each, the same four-move sentence shape repeated three times.

What we are doing

  1. Learn the response sentence: name, how it is used, effect, evidence.
  2. Read the worked timbre dot point and see the four parts at work.
  3. Meet the exam question: how are the elements of music used to create interest?
  4. Plan your own response: three elements, one dot point each.
  5. Write a full response and check each dot point against the four parts.
  6. Swap with a partner and underline the effect in each other's dot points.

Your task

Write a full response to the excerpt answering: how are the elements of music used to create interest? Cover three elements, one dot point each. This is exactly the shape of Part B of the exam, which is worth 6 of the 20 marks.

Before you leave

Underline the 'effect' part of each of your three dot points.
