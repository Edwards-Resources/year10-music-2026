Lesson 18

Lesson 18: Film music practical / compose

Lesson 18 of 28, in Week 7 of Sound Detectives.

Learning intention

I can use an element of music to create a mood for a scene.

Success criteria

tick them off as you go, they save on your device

Read this

Choose one element, decide what you want the audience to feel, then prove your cue actually does it.

Lesson 17 gave you the vocabulary a film composer works with: leitmotif, underscore, mickey-mousing. Today you are not identifying which trick someone else used. You are the composer, on a scene that currently has no music at all, and you have to make one small decision and defend it.

Pick one element, not five

A cue that tries to do everything at once - faster, louder, brighter, busier - usually ends up doing nothing clearly, because a listener cannot tell which change is carrying the meaning. Pick a single element and let it do the whole job.

  • Tempo - a speed you can hold steady is worth more than a speed you cannot. If you are not sure where to start, work at 80 to 100 beats a minute: slow enough to play accurately, fast enough to sound like a decision rather than a drift.
  • Dynamics - decide whether the scene needs a build, a drop, or one sudden change, and place it at a single moment rather than smearing it across the whole span.
  • Timbre - pick one sound and commit to it for the whole span. Swapping instruments halfway through is a different exercise from choosing a colour and sticking with it.

Working rule: name the effect you want in one sentence before you play a single note. “Something tense” is not a plan. “The tempo speeds up steadily across the span so the walk starts to feel like it cannot be stopped” is a plan, because it tells you exactly what a finished cue has to do to count as working.

A cue you have not tested against the picture is a guess wearing a finished cue's clothes.

Test it before you call it done

Finishing a cue and testing a cue are two different jobs, and it is easy to stop after the first one. Play your cue back against the muted clip, at the same time, and ask three questions:

  • Does the element you chose actually change the way you planned, the whole way through the span, or does it drift?
  • If someone who had not heard your one-sentence plan watched the clip with your cue under it, could they guess the mood without being told?
  • Is the effect landing at roughly the right moment in the span, or does it arrive too early or too late to match anything on screen?

Working rule: if the test fails, change the element you chose more, not a different element instead. A crescendo that does not read as tension usually needs to be slower and bigger, not swapped for a tempo change halfway through. One decisive change is easier for a listener to follow than several timid ones.

This is the same reasoning Lesson 19 uses on a professional cue, at a much bigger scale: name what changes, when, and what it buys the moment on screen. You are doing that job as the composer today. Next lesson you do it as the analyst, on someone else's finished decision.

Listen

“Sintel”, Blender Foundation (Creative Commons)

The film runs 14 minutes 48. **Watch it with the sound off**, and do not try to watch all of it: you are looking for one span of 20 to 30 seconds to score, and the next section tells you how to choose one. Nothing you write today has to fit anything but the span you pick.

Choose your span, before you touch an instrument

  1. Play the clip above with the sound off, and skim it, rather than sitting through all 14 minutes, before you decide anything.
  2. Choose one continuous span inside it, 20 to 30 seconds long, with a clear visual moment in it: a change of pace, a look, an object, a point where something on screen shifts.
  3. Write down the exact start and end time of your span before you touch an instrument. If you cannot say where it starts and ends, you cannot score it accurately, and no one checking your work can tell what you were scoring either.
  4. Write your one-sentence plan next to it: the element you are using and the effect you want.

Build it, in whichever tool you have

  1. flat.io: start a new score and set the tempo in the toolbar first, before you add a single note. Add one instrument track and sketch four to eight bars. Change your chosen element by editing that tempo number, the dynamic markings, or the instrument in the track settings, not all three.
  2. A DAW such as GarageBand: one software instrument track, a loop of four to eight bars. Use volume or velocity automation for a dynamics change, or swap the instrument patch for a timbre change. Keep the loop short - you are testing one idea, not producing a finished soundtrack.
  3. Instruments, live: agree the element and the effect as a group before anyone plays. Count yourselves in so the tempo is shared from the first beat, and run it through twice before you call it finished.

What we are doing

  1. Watch the muted clip and choose your span; write down its start and end time.
  2. Pick one element and write your one-sentence plan for the effect you want.
  3. Build a short cue in flat.io, a DAW or with instruments.
  4. Test the cue against the muted clip and adjust it if it does not land.
  5. Share your span, your element and your plan, and say whether the test confirmed it.

Your task

Create a cue for the exact span you chose and wrote down, 20 to 30 seconds long, using the one element from your plan. Be ready to state your span's start and end time, the element you used, and the effect you were testing for.

Before you leave

State your span, the element you used, and whether your test confirmed the effect you planned.

If you were away: the process, worked once
  • The order is fixed: choose your span and write it down, pick one element, write a one-sentence plan for the effect, build, then test. The tempo, the notes, the instrument and the mood you go for are yours; that order is not.
  • If you are stuck on a starting tempo, 80 to 100 beats a minute is a safe default. Slow enough to play accurately, fast enough to sound like a decision rather than a drift.
  • A test has to compare something, not just confirm the cue exists. Play it back against the muted clip and ask whether someone who has not heard your plan could guess the mood from it. If you cannot answer that, you have finished a cue, not tested one.
  • Worked example of the process, not an answer key, because there is no single right cue for a span only you chose: element = dynamics, plan = “a calm shot that quietly turns uneasy”, build = a slow crescendo from almost silent to just loud enough to notice, timed to peak about two thirds of the way through the span, test = played back muted, does the rising loudness read as tension building or as a mistake getting louder. If it reads as a mistake, the crescendo is too sudden for the span: stretch it across more of the time you have and test again.
