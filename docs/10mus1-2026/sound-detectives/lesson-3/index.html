Lesson 3: Writing about music 1

Lesson 3 of 28, in Week 1 of Sound Detectives.

Learning intention

I can write a clear sentence about how an element is used.

Success criteria

Tick them off as you go.

Learn

Writing about music is not saying what you liked. It is reporting what you heard.

A listening answer is not an essay. It is one short sentence, built on purpose, and the same four moves build every one.

The four moves, as four questions

  • Which element? Name it: the word for the element, out of the six from Lesson 1, not “it” and not “the music”.
  • What does it do that I can hear? The sound itself, close enough for a reader who has never played the track to picture it.
  • What does that do to the music? Not what it made you feel: what it does to the sound around it, or to where the song is heading.
  • Where did I hear it? A time, a section, or “the whole way through” when that is true.

Working rule: one sentence, one element. A sentence carrying two elements short-changes one of them, and the marker cannot tell which one you were answering. That is why listening answers are dot points and not a paragraph.

Your Lesson 1 notes are most of a sentence already: swap your word for the element word, keep the time you wrote down, and hedge honestly, because “about 95 beats a minute” is trusted and a decimal you never measured is not.

Learn

Name it, say what it does, say what that does, say where. Four moves, in that order.

Finding the effect, when nothing comes to mind

This is the move people stall on. Substitute instead: imagine the same track with that element used the other way, a tempo drifting about instead of holding steady, and ask what has gone missing. What you just named is the effect. Keep it about this track: anything true of every song ever recorded is not an effect at all.

The frame comes back in Lesson 9, where three of these sentences make a full response. For now, one sentence.

Listen

Worked example

Question Describe how one element of music is used in this excerpt. 2 marks

Watch this get built rather than reading it finished. It is assembled a move at a time, on duration. The rungs carry the ALARM names your other subjects use, and they are the same four moves as above in the same order: Identify is name it, Describe is how it is used, Explain is effect, and Evidence is evidence.

A full answer

The tempo stays at about 95 beats a minute and never moves, so the speed does nothing to mark one part of the track off from another and the moments that feel bigger are getting their weight from something other than hurry: count the beat at 0:20 and again at 2:20 and it comes out the same.

  1. Identify Name the element and nothing else yet. The tempo stays at about 95 beats a minute. Worth no marks alone, and that is the point: you have started the sentence, not finished it.
  2. Describe Say what it does across the excerpt. ... and never moves. Three words, and they turn a number into something you followed for the whole track.
  3. Explain Say what that does to the music. ... so the speed does nothing to mark one part of the track off from another. Nothing new was heard here: the hearing happened in the move before. This move is where the second mark lives.
  4. Evidence Point at the music so somebody else could check you. Count the beat at 0:20 and again at 2:20. Evidence need not be a timestamp, but it must be something a marker could test.
What scores nothing

“The song has a really good beat and the tempo is perfect for it.”

It names an element and then compliments it. No number, no place in the track, and “perfect” is a verdict rather than a description. The student heard something, then wrote down how they felt.

If you were away

If you were away: the four moves
  • Name - which of the six elements the sentence is about.
  • How it is used - what the element does, closely enough to be heard from your words.
  • Effect - what the music gains or loses from being that way. The move most often left out.
  • Evidence - the place in the music you are pointing at, so the claim can be checked.

In classWhat we are doing

  1. Learn the four moves: name, how it is used, effect, evidence.
  2. Watch one sentence get built, a move at a time.
  3. Copy the frame table into your book.
  4. Write two dot points, on two different elements.
  5. Swap with a partner and find the move they left out.

Your task

Write two dot points on “Watermelon Sugar”, on two different elements. Four moves in each, one element to a sentence. If the second stalls, build it from a line in your Lesson 1 notes.

Exit ticket

Underline the ‘effect’ part of your best sentence.

Check yourself

Check yourself: two sentences to fix
  1. Your note reads “2:12 the bottom comes back and it jumps louder”. Which two moves is it doing already? How it is used, and evidence. Missing are the element word and the effect.
  2. Which move is missing here? “At 0:52 the track gets about half again as loud, which is a big change.” The effect. It says how large the change is, not what the change does.

Slides and notes

  • This is the first teaching of the response sentence. Model it on the board a move at a time, the way the worked answer on the page does, and hold the class to one sentence: the full three-dot-point response is Lesson 9’s job.
  • Students have notes on this track from Lesson 1. Have those out before anyone starts writing, or the lesson turns back into a listening lesson.

Sound Detectives

Week 1: The Elements Toolkit

Welcome to our listening unit. This week we meet the six elements of music and learn to describe what we hear in clear, musical language.

  1. 1Active listening and the elements of musicI can name the elements of music and use them to describe what I hear.
  2. 2Duration and pitch (listen and play)I can identify duration and pitch features by ear.
  3. 3Writing about music 1I can write a clear sentence about how an element is used.

Week 2: Texture, Timbre and Comparing

This week we listen across styles to explore texture and timbre, and learn how to compare two works using the elements of music.

  1. 4Texture across styles (listen and build)I can identify and describe texture in different styles.
  2. 5Performing media and timbreI can identify performing media and describe timbre.
  3. 6Comparing “Valerie”I can compare two versions of the same song using performing media and timbre.

Week 3: Dynamics, Structure and Exam Writing

This week we finish the toolkit with dynamics, expression and structure, and start writing full responses to the exam question.

  1. 7Dynamics and expressionI can identify dynamics and expression and explain their effect.
  2. 8StructureI can identify the structure of a work by ear.
  3. 9Writing about music: the response frame and the exam questionI can write a full response on how the elements of music are used.

Week 4: Exam Preparation

One lesson this week, because the trial examinations compress the class to two periods and the AT3 examination follows on Wednesday of Week 5. We rehearse the whole exam in a single period: listening widely, annotating as we listen, and writing a full response under time.

  1. 10Exam preparation: wide listening, strategies and a timed responseI can use exam strategies and write to the standard of the AT3 task.

Week 5: AT3 Exam and Feedback

This week you sit the AT3 Listening Examination, then return to practical playing and review your responses to set goals.

  1. 11AT3 Listening ExaminationI can analyse how the elements of music are manipulated and combined.Assessment
  2. 12Practical recovery lessonI can contribute to a group performance with awareness.
  3. 13Exam feedback and reflectionI can evaluate my own listening response and set a goal.

Week 6: Jazz

This week we listen to jazz, play a 12-bar blues and improvise, then analyse how jazz uses the elements of music to create interest.

  1. 14Jazz listeningI can identify the features and context of jazz.
  2. 15Jazz practical: 12-bar bluesI can perform and improvise within a 12-bar blues.
  3. 16Analysing a jazz recordingI can analyse how a jazz recording’s register and dynamics change across a listening, using measured evidence.

Week 7: Film Music

This week we explore how film music creates mood and tells stories, create a short cue, and analyse a film composer’s choices.

  1. 17Film music listeningI can identify how film music supports narrative.
  2. 18Film music practical / composeI can use an element of music to create a mood for a scene.
  3. 19Analysing a film cueI can analyse how a composer builds a film cue using the elements of music.

Week 8: Music of Australia and First Nations Music

This week we listen to and analyse music of Australia, including music of Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander Peoples, with respect for context and protocols.

  1. 20Music of Australia and First Nations musicI can describe Australian music and its context respectfully.
  2. 21Australian music analysisI can analyse an Australian work using elements and context.
  3. 22Practical and response: Australian stylesI can perform a feature of an Australian style and describe it.

Week 9: Comparative and Critical Listening

This week we compare how styles use the elements differently, evaluate interpretive choices, and start thinking about the Term 4 Depth Study.

  1. 23Comparative listening across stylesI can compare how texture is used across three styles, using evidence from the recordings.
  2. 24Critical listening: evaluating choicesI can evaluate interpretive choices in performance.
  3. 25Student-choice listening and Depth Study sparkI can analyse music I choose and identify a personal area of interest.

Week 10: Synthesis and Depth Study Launch

This week we present our analyses, launch the Term 4 Depth Study, and finish with an approved proposal ready to develop.

  1. 26Synthesis presentationI can present a clear analysis of a work.
  2. 27Depth Study launch and idea generationI can generate realistic Depth Study ideas that match my interests and skills.
  3. 28Depth Study proposal (milestone) and term celebrationI can write a clear, realistic plan for my Depth Study.
