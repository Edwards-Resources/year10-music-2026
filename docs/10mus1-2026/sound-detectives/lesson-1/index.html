Lesson 1

Lesson 1: Active listening and the elements of music

Lesson 1 of 28, in Week 1 of Sound Detectives.

Learning intention

I can name the elements of music and use them to describe what I hear.

Success criteria

tick them off as you go, they save on your device

Read this

Everyone can hear music. Listening means hearing it in categories.

You can love a song for years and still not be able to say one useful thing about how it works. That is not a failure of taste. Hearing and listening are different jobs: hearing happens on its own, and listening is deliberate. It is also a skill, which means it improves quickly once you know what you are actually doing.

The method, and it is only four moves

  • Pick one question. Not the song, not the vibe. One of the six on this page, and only that one.
  • Play the excerpt and answer only that question. Anything else you notice, let go of. It will still be there on the next play.
  • Write what you heard, when it happened, and what it did. A time and a verb. “At 0:52 the bottom of the sound arrives and the whole track gets heavier” is a note you can use later. “Sounds good” is not.
  • Play it again with the next question. Six questions, six passes. In an examination you get three plays, so you spend them on the questions the marks are actually for.

Working rule: one question per playing. Trying to hear everything at once is how you end up hearing nothing, and it is the single most common reason a listening answer comes out as “it builds up and it sounds really good”. Play a track listening only for texture and you will hear things you missed on fifty previous plays. That is not a trick, only attention pointed at one place instead of six.

Every listening question you will be asked, this term and in every examination after it, names one element and asks about that one. Practise hearing them separately and the question has already told you where to listen.

The six questions

The music changes every lesson. The six questions never do.

Here they are, each with a test you can run on any track in any style, without knowing anything about it in advance. The tests matter more than the definitions: a definition you can recite is worth very little in a listening room.

Duration: how does it move through time?

Tap your foot. What you are tapping is the beat, and how quickly you are tapping is the tempo. Count your taps for fifteen seconds and multiply by four, and you have a number you can defend rather than a guess. Then stop tapping and follow the tune instead: the pattern of long and short notes in the tune is the rhythm, and it almost never lines up with your foot. That mismatch is the point of it.

Pitch: how high or low, and what does the tune do?

Two questions in one. Where the sound sits, high or low, is its register: try to sing along and notice whether you are straining upwards or growling downwards. What the tune does over time is its contour, which climbs, falls, arches or stays put. Nobody is asking you to name the notes by ear, only to describe the movement.

Texture: how many layers, and is anything holding the bottom?

Try to point at the separate things happening at the same time. One voice alone is thin. A voice with a guitar under it is thicker. A full band with something low holding the bottom is thicker again. The most useful version of this question is also the easiest one to answer: is there anything underneath, or is the bottom of the sound empty?

Performing media and timbre: what is making the sound, and what is that sound like?

Performing media is the list of what is playing: which voices, which instruments, how many. Timbre is the quality of the sound itself, and it needs an adjective you could defend to somebody who disagrees with you: bright, warm, breathy, buzzy, hollow, thin, rich. This is the one question on the list nothing can answer for you, which is why it rewards knowing the names of instruments. Start collecting them.

Dynamics and expression: how loud, and does the loudness change?

Loud and soft, everything between, and what the performer does to shape a note on top of that: an accent, a swell, a note left hanging. The test is a volume knob in your head. Set it at the loudest moment of the excerpt, then ask where the quietest moment sits against it: most of the way down, or barely below? Be careful here, because a passage can sound smaller without getting any softer, and writing “it gets quieter” when nothing got quieter is the easiest mark on this page to throw away.

Structure: what is the shape of the whole thing?

This is the only one of the six you cannot hear in a single second. It exists across time: it is the pattern you notice when you compare what you are hearing now against what you heard a minute ago. What you listen for is boundaries, the places where one section stops and another starts. Working rule: if two or more of the other elements change at the same moment, that moment is a boundary. One element changing on its own is usually decoration inside a section rather than the start of a new one.

Six questions. Every piece of music ever written answers all six, including the pieces you would never choose to listen to. That is exactly why they are worth having.

Listen

Harry Styles, “Watermelon Sugar”2:53 · Harry Styles

Play it once, all the way through, before you read a single cue. Write down anything you notice, in any words you like. That first list is yours and nobody marks it. Then come back to the top and work the seven cues below, one at a time, clicking a time to jump the player to it. The cues work through five of the six questions in turn, and the note under them says which one is missing and why.

  1. 0:00Start with texture, because the opening makes it easy. Listen for what is underneath the sound. Nothing is: the bottom stays empty for the first twelve seconds or so, so what you are hearing is a thin texture with a hole under it. You have just made a texture judgement in one second, without knowing the name of a single instrument on the track.
  2. 0:52The biggest arrival in the first half of the track, and it is worth working out why it feels that big. Two things happen in the same second: the level jumps by about half again, and the bottom of the sound arrives underneath it, four to six times what it had been. Two elements moving together, which by the working rule above makes this a boundary. Write down what changed. Do not write down what the section is called, because nothing you can hear from here proves that.
  3. 1:00Now duration, and you need your foot for this one. Tap the beat, count fifteen seconds of taps, multiply by four, and write the number down before you read anything else. The same count works back at 0:10 and it works here, which is what it means to say the tempo does not change: the arrival at 0:52 made the track heavier, not faster. Those are different elements and they are easy to confuse.
  4. 1:50Dynamics. From here the track empties out, and this is the only time after the opening that it does. Follow the level down across the next six seconds and try to hold on to how far it falls, because you will need it for the worked answer further down the page.
    1. 1:52The first step down, and it is a step rather than a fade: about three quarters of what it was, arriving all at once.
    2. 1:56The second step, and the bottom of the sound goes with it. By here the track is under a third as loud as it was six seconds ago, which makes this the quietest it has been since the opening.
  5. 2:06Pitch, and this is the trap the last question warned you about. Everything sounding across the next five seconds is in the top half of the range, with nothing at all underneath it. It is not quiet: the level here is roughly where it was before. So if you write that it gets quieter here, you have named the wrong element and the mark goes with it. Work out which element it actually is before you open the answers at the bottom of the page.
    1. 2:08The emptiest second of the passage. There is effectively nothing in the bottom of the sound at all, and the energy has moved up into the top of the range instead.
  6. 2:12The bottom comes back, and the track jumps about forty per cent louder in the same second. Two elements moving at once again, exactly like 0:52. Name both of them in your book before you move on. From here to the end nothing collapses again, so this is the last big move the track makes.
  7. 2:44Structure last, because it is the one question you can only answer once you have heard the whole thing. Follow the ending from here and time it: the track is still at full level at 2:50 and there is nothing left by 2:52, so it stops rather than fades. Now draw the track as a line, left to right, and mark the four big moves on it: 0:52, 1:50 to 1:56, 2:06, and 2:12. That line is a structure answer, and you drew it without needing the word verse or the word chorus once.

Notice what these cues never do: not one of them tells you which instruments are playing. Every timestamp on this page was measured off this exact recording, and a measurement can say how loud a moment is and which part of the range is sounding, but it cannot say what is making the sound. That is the one question on the list your ears have to answer on their own, and it gets a lesson of its own later in the unit.

If you were away: what each element asks
  • Duration - speed and time: how fast it goes, and how the notes are grouped and spaced. Beat, tempo, metre and rhythm all live under this one.
  • Pitch - high and low: where the sound sits in the range, and what the tune does over time. Register, contour and range live here.
  • Texture - how many layers are sounding at once, and how they sit against each other. Thick or thin, and whether anything is holding the bottom.
  • Performing media and timbre - what is making the sound, and what that sound is like. The voices and instruments, and the words for their colour: bright, warm, breathy, buzzy.
  • Dynamics and expression - how loud it is, and how the loudness changes. Loud, soft, sudden, gradual, plus what a performer does to shape a note.
  • Structure - the order of the sections, and the shape of the whole thing. The only one of the six that needs more than a second of music before you can answer it.

What we are doing

  1. Listen to the track once with nothing to do but notice, and jot down anything you hear.
  2. Meet the six elements of music: duration, pitch, texture, performing media and timbre, dynamics and expression, structure.
  3. Fill in the elements table on this page as we meet each one.
  4. Add each term to your listening glossary.
  5. Work through the cues on the track, one element at a time.
  6. Listen again and tag what you hear to an element.

Your task

Pick three of the six elements and write one line each about this track: what you heard, the time it happened, and what it does to the music. Three lines, not three paragraphs.

Worked answer

Identify one element of music in this excerpt and describe how it is used. (2 marks)

This is the shape of almost every short listening question you will meet this term, and it is today's task in the same words. Here is one full answer, on the passage you followed at 1:50.

From 1:50 the dynamics fall away in two steps until, by 1:56, the track is under a third as loud as it was and quieter than it has been since the opening, and that emptiness is what makes the full sound at 2:12 land as an arrival rather than as more of the same.

  • IdentifyName the element and say where it is. Dynamics, from 1:50. “The dynamics change” names an element but no change and no place, and earns nothing at all.
  • DescribeSay what it sounds like and how far it goes. Falling away in two steps to under a third as loud, quieter than anything since the opening. A marker should be able to hear it from your words without playing the track.
  • ExplainSay what it does to the music. That emptiness makes the return at 2:12 land as an arrival. This is what the second mark is for, and it is the half most people leave off.

What scores nothing

“The dynamics in this song are really effective and add a lot to the mood.”

A whole sentence of nothing. It names an element and then says the element is good, which is true of every piece of music ever recorded and therefore says nothing about this one. No time, no sound, no effect. A short answer is fine here. An empty one is not.

Before you leave

Name three elements you heard today.

Check yourself: four questions on today
  1. You counted the beat at 1:00. What number should you have written down, and does it change? About 95 beats a minute, and no, it does not change anywhere in the track. Anything from 92 to 98 means you counted rather than guessed.
  2. Two elements move at the same second at 0:52. Which two? Dynamics, because the whole thing gets louder, and texture, because a layer arrives underneath that was not there before. You could argue pitch as well, since what arrives is low, and you would not be wrong. The mark is for hearing that two things moved together, which is what makes the moment a boundary.
  3. From 2:06 the track is not quiet, but something has gone. Which element is that a statement about? Pitch, or register if you want to be exact: everything sounding is in the top half of the range, with nothing under it. It is not a dynamics answer, because the level barely moved, and that is the most common wrong answer on this page.
  4. Name three elements you heard today, with one word for how each is used. Any three of: texture, thin at the start; dynamics, sudden at 0:52; duration, steady the whole way through; pitch, high from 2:06; structure, built around four big moves at 0:52, 1:50, 2:06 and 2:12. The sixth is performing media and timbre, and nothing on this page measured that one for you, so if you named an instrument you heard it yourself.
