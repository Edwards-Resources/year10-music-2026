Lesson 2: Duration and pitch (listen and play)

Lesson 2 of 28, in Week 1 of Sound Detectives.

Learning intention

I can identify duration and pitch features by ear.

Success criteria

Tick them off as you go.

Learn

Beat, tempo, metre and rhythm are four different things, and only one of them is your foot.

These four get mixed up more than any other words in music, and it costs marks, because writing “the rhythm speeds up” when you mean the tempo speeds up tells the marker you cannot hear the difference. You do not separate them by memorising four definitions. You separate them by testing them against each other on a real track.

Four tests, on any track you like

  • Tap your foot. What you are tapping is the beat. It keeps going underneath whether anyone plays a note on it or not.
  • Count how quickly you are tapping. That is the tempo.
  • Notice that some taps feel stronger than others. Strong, weak, weak. Strong, weak, weak. That grouping is the metre, and it is why some music feels like it is in threes and some in fours.
  • Now follow the tune instead of your foot. The pattern of long and short notes in the tune is the rhythm, and it almost never lines up exactly with your tapping. That mismatch is the point of it.

Working rule: tempo and rhythm are independent. Play the same rhythm twice as fast and it is still the same rhythm, just quicker. If you can change one without touching the other, they are not the same thing, and these are the two students collapse into one most often.

Pitch: describe the motion, not the notes

Nobody is asking you to name notes by ear. You are being asked to describe movement. A melody’s contour is its shape over time, whether it climbs, falls, arches or waves. Its range is how far it reaches from its lowest note to its highest, which is a separate question: a melody can have a dramatic shape inside a narrow range, or barely move across a wide one. When it moves a small distance to the next note it has stepped, and when it jumps a long way it has leapt. Shape, distance, and how far the whole thing spans. That is the level of detail a listening answer wants.

Key words

Duration
How music moves through time: is it fast or slow, are the notes short or long, does it have a steady beat or does it feel off-kilter?
Pitch
How high or low the notes are, and what the melody does: does it climb, fall, stay put?

Learn

Count the groups and you have found the metre.

Metre is how the beats are grouped, so the quickest way to find it is to count. Pick out the strong pulses and count from one strong pulse to the next. Groups of two feel like a march. Groups of three feel like a waltz.

Simple and compound

  • Simple metre: each beat splits into two. 2/4, 3/4 and 4/4 are simple.
  • Compound metre: each beat splits into three. 6/8 is compound: two big beats, each made of three quick ones.

Six quick clicks can be either. Group them 3 + 3 and you feel two big beats that swing, which is 6/8. Group them 2 + 2 + 2 and you feel three beats that march, which is 3/4. Same six clicks, different metre. In class we count both on the click tracks, which all run at the same speed, so the grouping is the only thing that changes.

Listen

In classWhat we are doing

  1. Recap: duration = beat, tempo, metre, rhythm. Pitch = melody, contour, range.
  2. Write the definitions of duration and pitch in your books.
  3. Fill in the duration, tempo and pitch tables on this page as we work through them.
  4. Count the groups on the metre click tracks: 2s, 3s, then six clicks grouped two ways.
  5. Clap or play the rhythm patterns as a class.
  6. Play or sing the short melodies and describe their shape.
  7. Listen and identify these features by ear.

Your task

Notate or describe a 4-bar rhythm you hear, then describe the contour of a short melody (rising, falling, wave).

Exit ticket

Was the melody’s range wide or narrow? How do you know?
